1. Preface

1.1 Introduction

One of the most widely discussed topics within Hinduism is the status of meat, or māṃsa, because of both its great relevance to the daily lives of more than a billion people and its highly controversial nature. The heated disagreements are mainly between three groups.

First are the strict vegetarians, who allege that Hinduism completely bans meat-eating. They base this view on many verses that speak of ahiṃsā (minimal violence), condemn meat, and glorify abstinence. The second group takes the opposite position. Pointing to such things in scripture as animal sacrifice, hunting, and meat-eating by revered personalities, it says that Hinduism in fact permits all meat. Neither group is willing to engage with the other’s references, instead characterising them as later developments, corruptions, or misinterpretations.

The third group sees all this and concludes that Hindu scriptures must simply be contradictory: different texts, written at different times by different people, which can be used to justify any position at all.

This work belongs to none of these three groups. Without ignoring any references, it will show that the scriptures have a clear and consistent view, using only the primary sources themselves. In the second half, it will also address several adjacent questions.

1.2 On pramāṇas

Before attempting to prove anything, we must discuss how something is to be proven at all: our epistemology. The scope of this work is not to discuss the economic, nutritional, or any other such aspect of meat. It is only to clarify what Hinduism says about it. In that regard, there is only one valid epistemic source. Lord Kṛṣṇa states in the Gītā:

Show Sanskrit

यः शास्त्रविधिमुत्सृज्य वर्तते कामकारतः। न स सिद्धिमवाप्नोति न सुखं न परां गतिम्॥
तस्माच्छास्त्रं प्रमाणं ते कार्याकार्यव्यवस्थितौ। ज्ञात्वा शास्त्रविधानोक्तं कर्म कर्तुमिहार्हसि॥

The Supreme Lord said: “He who discards scriptural procedure and proceeds according to his own wishes attains neither success, happiness, nor the highest goal. Therefore, scripture is your basis for determining what is and is not to be done. Having understood the scriptural injunction, it befits you to perform actions in this world.”

—Bhagavad Gītā 16.23–24

Similarly, the Mīmāṃsā Sūtras, which contain maxims for scriptural interpretation, state in their second aphorism:

Show Sanskrit

चोदनालक्षणोऽर्थो धर्मः॥ १.१.२॥

“That which is enjoined by a statement of scripture is dharma.”

Because meat is a matter of dharma and kāryākāryavyavasthiti, this work will treat scripture as its sole basis. Here, scripture means the Vedas and the texts endorsed by the Vedas: the Manusmṛti, the itihāsas (the Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa), and the Purāṇas.

1.3 Summary

The synthesis of all scriptural injunctions regarding meat-eating, as this document will demonstrate, is as follows.

Pūrvārdha: the main thesis

  1. The general rule (utsarga) is that meat is forbidden because it violates ahiṃsā.
  2. In certain ritual contexts—including Vedic yajña, śrāddha (ancestral rites), tantric rituals, brāhmaṇa-bhoga, and hunting by kṣatriyas—meat-eating is permitted. This is an exception (apavāda) that does not undermine the general rule.
  3. Although meat is permitted in these exceptional cases, it is not encouraged; the scriptures highly praise complete abstention from it.

Uttarārdha: related questions

  1. According to some ācāryas, another exception is the local practice (deśācāra) of certain regions, such as eating fish in Bengal; this is disputed. Although the nutritional value of meat is recognised, it is not a valid exception.
  2. The scriptures strictly forbid beef, even within the exceptional contexts. They also discourage animals such as domesticated pigs, chickens, and camels while preferring animals such as goats, wild boars, and deer.
  3. The Rāmāyaṇa clearly states that Lord Rāma consumed meat under the hunting exception.

2. Pūrvārdha

2.1 Condemnation of meat-eating

The two most important places in scripture for this topic are Manusmṛti Chapter 5 and Mahābhārata 13.116–117. Indeed, a thorough reading of these two chapters alone would allow anyone to understand the Hindu view of meat. In this document, they will be presented side by side, in a clear progression, alongside parallel verses and other pramāṇas.

Show Sanskrit

नाकृत्वा प्राणिनां हिंसां मांसमुत्पद्यते क्वचित्। न च प्राणिवधः स्वर्ग्यस्तस्मान्मांसं विवर्जयेत्॥

“Meat is never obtained without the killing of animals. The killing of animals does not lead to heaven; therefore, one should avoid meat.”

—Manusmṛti 5.48

Show Sanskrit

अहिंसा परमो धर्मस्तथाहिंसा परं तपः। अहिंसा परमं सत्यं ततो धर्मः प्रवर्तते॥
न हि मांसं तृणात्काष्ठादुपलाद्वापि जायते। हत्वा जन्तुं ततो मांसं तस्माद्दोषोऽस्य भक्षणे॥

“Ahiṃsā is the highest dharma; ahiṃsā is the greatest austerity. Ahiṃsā is the highest truth, from which dharma proceeds. Meat is not produced from grass, wood, or stone; it is obtained only by killing a living being. Therefore, there is fault in eating it.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116.25–26

These verses clearly establish the principle: meat is forbidden because it can be obtained only by killing animals, which violates ahiṃsā.

These chapters next explain the etymology of the word māṃsa (“meat”) and the karmic consequences of eating it.

Show Sanskrit

मां स भक्षयितामुत्र यस्य मांसमिहाद्म्यहम्। एतन्मांसस्य मांसत्वं प्रवदन्ति मनीषिणः॥

“‘Me he (māṃ-sa) will devour in the next world, whose meat I eat in this world’: this is the ‘meatness’ (māṃsatva) of meat (māṃsa), as the wise declare.”

—Manusmṛti 5.55

Show Sanskrit

ये भक्षयन्ति मांसानि भूतानां जीवितैषिणाम्। भक्ष्यन्ते तेऽपि तैर्भूतैरिति मे नास्ति संशयः॥
मां स भक्षयते यस्माद्भक्षयिष्ये तमप्यहम्। एतन्मांसस्य मांसत्वमतो बुध्यस्व भारत॥
घातको वध्यते नित्यं तथा वध्येत बन्धकः। आक्रोष्टाक्रुश्यते राजन् द्वेष्टा द्वेष्यमवाप्नुते॥
येन येन शरीरेण यद्यत्कर्म करोति यः। तेन तेन शरीरेण तत्तत्फलमुपाश्नुते॥

“Those who eat the meat of living beings are themselves eaten by those beings in the afterlife; of this I have no doubt. ‘Me he (māṃ sa) has eaten, therefore I too shall eat him’: understand this, O descendant of Bharata, as the essence of the word māṃsa. The killer is slain, and the one who binds the animal is also slain. The reviler is reviled, and the hater is hated. Whatever actions one performs through a body, through such a body one experiences the corresponding consequences.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116.34–35

Both texts give the same wordplay. The sentence “Me he has eaten; I shall eat him too” begins with “me he” (māṃ sa), which is stated to be the essence of the word māṃsa. The Mahābhārata goes one step further and explains the general karmic principle—not merely in relation to meat, but to all actions—that whatever one does, one must face in return.

The chapter then gives a long praise of ahiṃsā.

Show Sanskrit

अहिंसा परमो धर्मस्तथाहिंसा परो दमः। अहिंसा परमं दानमहिंसा परमं तपः॥
अहिंसा परमो यज्ञस्तथाहिंसा परं बलम्। अहिंसा परमं मित्रमहिंसा परमं सुखम्॥
अहिंसा परमं सत्यमहिंसा परमं श्रुतम्। सर्वयज्ञेषु वा स्नानं सर्वतीर्थेषु चाप्लुतम्॥
सर्वदानफलं वापि नैतत्तुल्यमहिंसया। अहिंस्रस्य तपोऽक्षय्यमहिंस्रो यजते सदा॥
अहिंस्रः सर्वभूतानां यथा माता यथा पिता॥

“Ahiṃsā is the highest dharma and the greatest self-control. Ahiṃsā is the highest charity and the greatest austerity. Ahiṃsā is the highest yajña and the greatest strength. Ahiṃsā is the best friend and the greatest happiness. Ahiṃsā is the highest truth and the greatest revelation. Bathing at all sacred places, performing every yajña, and giving every form of charity do not equal ahiṃsā. The austerity of the nonviolent person is everlasting; the nonviolent person performs yajña continually and is like a mother and father to all beings.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116.37–40

The point is clear. Dharma rests on many principles, including satya (truthfulness) and dama (self-control), but the greatest of these—the one encompassing them all—is ahiṃsā (minimal violence). Meat, because it violates ahiṃsā, is therefore forbidden.

The chapter further recounts the story of King Vasu and how he fell from heaven after declaring meat fit to be eaten.

Show Sanskrit

ऋषिभिः संशयं पृष्टो वसुश्चेदिपतिः पुरा। अभक्ष्यमिति मांसं स प्राह भक्ष्यमिति प्रभो॥
आकाशान्मेदिनीं प्राप्तस्ततः स पृथिवीपतिः। एतदेव पुनश्चोक्त्वा विवेश धरणीतलम्॥

“Once, the ṛṣis questioned Vasu, king of Cedi, about meat. He declared meat—which is unfit to be eaten—to be fit for consumption. At that instant the king fell from heaven to the earth; after repeating the statement, he sank into the earth itself.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116.44–45

2.2 Exceptions in ritual contexts and hunting

Some strict vegetarians, relying on these pramāṇas, assume a blanket prohibition on all meat. They are then unable to account for the clear references to animal sacrifice in the Vedas, such as the following.

Show Sanskrit

होतारौ यक्षदश्विनौ छागस्य वपाया मेदसो जुषेताम्।

“The priest worships the Aśvins with the omentum fat of the goat.”

—Yajurveda 22.42

Show Sanskrit

यन्मन्थं यमोदनं यन्मांसं निपृणामि ते। ते ते सन्तु स्वधावन्तो मधुमन्तो घृतश्चुतः॥

“That mixture, that cooked meat which I offer to you—may it be sweet, filled with ghee, and accompanied by svadhā.”

—Atharvaveda 8.4.42

Another Vedic passage is quoted by Rāmānuja in his Gītā-bhāṣya on 2.3:

Show Sanskrit

अग्नीषोमीयादिषु च न हिंसा पशोः। हीनतरच्छागादिदेहपरित्यागपूर्वककल्याणदेहस्वर्गादिप्रापकत्वश्रुतेः संज्ञपनस्य।
न वा उ एतन्म्रियसे न रिष्यसि देवाँ इदेषि पथिभिः सुगेभिः। यत्र यान्ति सुकृतो नापि दुष्कृतस्तत्र त्वा देवः सविता दधातु॥

“There is no injury to the animal in rites such as the Agnīṣomīya. By relinquishing a lower body, such as that of a goat, the animal attains a superior body and heaven. This is also stated in the śruti: ‘Go forth; by this you do not perish, nor are you harmed. You go to the gods by auspicious and easy paths. Where the righteous, and not the evildoers, go—there may the god Savitṛ establish you.’”

Among many other pramāṇas, strict vegetarians may attempt to reinterpret, ignore, or question the authenticity of such passages, while nonvegetarians may take them as a licence to eat meat in all circumstances. Both approaches are wrong.

The correct interpretive framework is explained by Śaṅkara in his Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya 3.1.25:

Show Sanskrit

ननु “न हिंस्यात्सर्वभूतानि” इति शास्त्रमेव भूतविषयां हिंसामधर्म इत्यवगमयति। बाढम्—उत्सर्गस्तु सः; अपवादोऽयम् “अग्नीषोमीयं पशुमालभेत” इति। उत्सर्गापवादयोश्च व्यवस्थितविषयत्वम्। तस्माद्विशुद्धं कर्म वैदिकम्।

“The scripture enjoins, ‘One should not kill any being,’ and thus teaches that violence against beings is adharma. Certainly. But that is the general rule (utsarga), while this is the exception (apavāda): ‘Let him sacrifice the animal for Agnīṣoma.’ The distinct scopes of a general rule and its exception are well known. Therefore, animal sacrifice is a pure Vedic act.”

In Mīmāṃsā, a broad injunction such as ahiṃsā is the utsarga; a narrower injunction, such as the command to sacrifice an animal in a particular ritual, is the apavāda. The latter is treated as a specific exception to the general rule. The same interpretive pattern is found in grammar and other subjects.

In the case of meat, however, this framework need not be imposed from outside: the scriptures themselves state it clearly. Meat is forbidden by the principle of ahiṃsā, but the apparent violence of a Vedic animal sacrifice is not considered hiṃsā at all.

Show Sanskrit

या वेदविहिता हिंसा नियतास्मिंश्चराचरे। अहिंसामेव तां विद्याद्वेदाद्धर्मो हि निर्बभौ॥

“The violence prescribed by the Vedas for this world of moving and unmoving beings should be understood as ahiṃsā, for dharma has issued from the Veda.”

—Manusmṛti 5.44

As discussed earlier, dharma is whatever the scriptures—most notably the Vedas—enjoin. Ahiṃsā is dharma, and what the Vedas declare is dharma; therefore, acts declared by the Vedas to be dharmic are, even when they outwardly resemble violence, regarded as ahiṃsā.

The word ahiṃsā itself is worth discussing. The prefix a-, properly called nañ, has several meanings, as stated in the following verse:

Show Sanskrit

तत्सादृश्यमभावश्च तदन्यत्वं तदल्पता। अप्राशस्त्यं विरोधश्च नञर्थाः षट् प्रकीर्तिताः॥

“The six meanings of nañ are similarity, absence (abhāva), separation, minimisation (alpatā), non-commendability, and opposition.”

Two meanings are especially relevant here: abhāva (complete absence) and alpa (minimal or least). Although the former is usually assumed, the latter is more accurate in this context. Ahiṃsā does not mean an absolute ban on all violence, but minimal violence: refraining from unnecessary harm to any being. Actions enjoined by scripture—such as defending one’s country, self-defence, or a king’s punishment of criminals—are considered necessary and therefore do not violate ahiṃsā. Scriptural injunctions involving animal sacrifice or the consumption of meat fall under the same principle.

A similar principle appears in the Devī Bhāgavatam.

Show Sanskrit

कथं मुक्तिप्रदो धर्मो वेदोक्तो बत भूपते॥
प्रत्यक्षेण त्वनाचारः सोमपानं नराधिप। पशूनां हिंसनं तद्वद्भक्षणं चामिषस्य च॥
जनक उवाच—
हिंसा यज्ञेषु प्रत्यक्षा साहिंसा परिकीर्तिता। उपाधियोगतो हिंसा नान्यथेति विनिर्णयः॥

Śuka asked: “O king, how can the liberation-granting dharma taught in the Vedas appear immoral in practice, with such acts as drinking soma, killing animals, and eating their meat?”

—Devī Bhāgavatam 1.18.49–50, 57–58

Janaka replied: “That which appears to be hiṃsā in Vedic rituals is in fact ahiṃsā. It is called hiṃsā only in a qualified or nominal sense; it is not otherwise hiṃsā.”

We may now examine the verses that make Vedic sacrifice an exception to the prohibition on meat, beginning with the same two chapters in which that prohibition appeared.

Show Sanskrit

यज्ञाय जग्धिर्मांसस्येत्येष दैवो विधिः स्मृतः। अतोऽन्यथा प्रवृत्तिस्तु राक्षसो विधिरुच्यते॥

“The eating of meat for sacrifices is declared to be the divine rule; conduct contrary to this is described as demonic practice.”

—Manusmṛti 5.31

Show Sanskrit

यज्ञार्थं पशवः सृष्टाः स्वयमेव स्वयम्भुवा। यज्ञोऽस्य भूत्यै सर्वस्य तस्माद्यज्ञे वधोऽवधः॥

“Animals were created by the Self-born for Vedic rituals. Because yajña benefits all beings, killing performed in yajña is not killing at all.”

—Manusmṛti 5.39

The Mahābhārata states the same principle.

Show Sanskrit

पितृदैवतयज्ञेषु प्रोक्षितं हविरुच्यते। विधिना वेददृष्टेन तद्भुक्त्वेह न दुष्यति॥
यज्ञार्थे पशवः सृष्टा इत्यपि श्रूयते श्रुतिः। अतोऽन्यथा प्रवृत्तानां राक्षसो विधिरुच्यते॥

“What is consecrated in rites for the ancestors and deities, according to the proper Vedic procedure, is called havis, and one is not polluted by eating it. The śruti also states that animals were created for Vedic rituals. Conduct apart from this is described as demonic practice.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116.5

These verses first establish the permissibility of animal sacrifice and consumption within yajña, citing the same śruti that says animals were created for yajña. The Manusmṛti says yajña is “for the benefit of all,” alluding to the teaching that a sacrificed animal attains a higher realm. The Mahābhārata also clarifies that what is sacrificed is called havis, not māṃsa, the latter being associated with karmic consequences.

Second, the texts make clear that this is only an exception (apavāda), not the general rule (utsarga), because they condemn meat-eating outside this exception as “demonic practice.”

Show Sanskrit

हविर्यत्संस्कृतं मन्त्रैः प्रोक्षिताभ्युक्षितं शुचि। वेदोक्तेन प्रमाणेन पितृणां प्रक्रियासु च। अतोऽन्यथा वृथामांसमभक्ष्यं मनुरब्रवीत्॥

“That which is properly consecrated by mantras, purified according to the Vedic procedure, or used in rites for the ancestors, is pure havis. Anything else is vain meat, which Manu declared unfit to be eaten.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116.50

Ritual exceptions to the prohibition are also supported in the Purāṇas.

Show Sanskrit

यद् घ्राणभक्षो विहितः सुरायास्तथा पशोरालभनं न हिंसा। एवं व्यवायः प्रजया न रत्या इमं विशुद्धं न विदुः स्वधर्मम्॥
ये त्वनेवंविदोऽसन्तः स्तब्धाः सदभिमानिनः। पशून् द्रुह्यन्ति विश्रब्धाः प्रेत्य खादन्ति ते च तान्॥

“The smelling of wine is prescribed, and the ritual offering of an animal is not hiṃsā. Similarly, intercourse is for the conception of a child, not for pleasure. Those impious and arrogant men who do not understand this pure dharma confidently harm animals; after death, those animals eat them.”

—Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.5.13–14

The Bhāgavata states that acts such as smelling wine, eating meat, and sexual intercourse have prescribed contexts in which they are lawful—ritual contexts for the first two and conception for the third. Those who perform these acts outside their prescribed contexts face the karmic consequences; one who kills an animal outside a ritual context is eaten by that animal in the afterlife.

Show Sanskrit

मांसाशनं ये कुर्वन्ति तैः कार्यं पशुहिंसनम्। महिषाजवराहाणां बलिदानं विशिष्यते॥
देव्यग्रे निहता यान्ति पशवः स्वर्गमव्ययम्। न हिंसा पशुजा तत्र निघ्नतां तत्कृतेऽनघ॥
अहिंसा याज्ञिकी प्रोक्ता सर्वशास्त्रविनिर्णये॥

“Those who eat meat may perform animal sacrifice, especially the sacrifice of a buffalo, goat, or wild boar. Animals sacrificed before the Goddess attain everlasting heaven. No hiṃsā arises from the animal’s death in that act, O sinless one. The consensus of all scriptures declares the ahiṃsā of yajña.”

—Devī Bhāgavatam 3.26.32–34

It is notable that sacrifice “before the Goddess” refers not to a Vedic sacrifice but to a tantric one. In addition to sacrifices in Vedic, ancestral, and tantric contexts, the Mahābhārata adds the exception of brāhmaṇa-bhoga, feeding brāhmaṇas.

Show Sanskrit

अप्रोक्षितं वृथामांसं विधिहीनं न भक्षयेत्। भक्षयन्निरयं याति नरो नास्त्यत्र संशयः॥
प्रोक्षिताभ्युक्षितं मांसं तथा ब्राह्मणकाम्यया। अल्पदोषमिह ज्ञेयं विपरीते तु लिप्यते॥

“One should not eat meat that has not been consecrated and is devoid of proper procedure. A person who eats it goes to hell; of this there is no doubt. Meat properly consecrated, or prepared for feeding brāhmaṇas, entails only minimal fault; one is tainted by acting otherwise.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116.47–48

Finally, the Manusmṛti summarises all the ritual exceptions, together with the exception of an emergency, in a single verse.

Show Sanskrit

प्रोक्षितं भक्षयेन्मांसं ब्राह्मणानां च काम्यया। यथाविधि नियुक्तस्तु प्राणानामेव चात्यये॥

“One may eat meat that has been properly consecrated, at the wish of brāhmaṇas, when duly appointed according to rule, or when one’s life is in danger.”

—Manusmṛti 5.27

Hunting

We now come to the final exception: hunting. Unlike the exceptions already discussed, this is not technically a ritual context, but its justification is tied to ritual exceptions.

Show Sanskrit

आरण्याः सर्वदैवत्याः प्रोक्षिताः सर्वशो मृगाः। अगस्त्येन पुरा राजन् मृगया येन पूज्यते॥
राजर्षयः सर्व एव मृगयां यान्ति भारत। लिप्यन्ते न हि दोषेण न चैतत्पातकं विदुः॥

“In ancient times, O king, all forest animals were consecrated by the great sage Agastya. Therefore all royal sages hunt deer without being tainted, for it is not known to be a sin.”

—Mahābhārata 13.117.17–19

The Manusmṛti also alludes to this.

Show Sanskrit

यज्ञार्थं ब्राह्मणैर्वध्याः प्रशस्ता मृगपक्षिणः। भृत्यानां चैव वृत्त्यर्थमगस्त्यो ह्याचरत्पुरा॥

“Brāhmaṇas may kill the permitted animals and birds for yajña and for the sustenance of dependants, as Agastya did in ancient times.”

—Manusmṛti 5.22

The Bhāgavata likewise permits hunting for kṣatriyas, while specifying that it should not be excessive.

Show Sanskrit

तीर्थेषु प्रतिदृष्टेषु राजा मेध्यान् पशून् वने। यावदर्थमलं लुब्धो हन्यादिति नियम्यते॥

“The rule is that, in sacred places and according to scriptural injunction, a king may hunt forest animals fit for sacrifice, but only as many as are required and without greed.”

—Bhāgavata Purāṇa 4.26.10

2.3 Abstention even from lawful meat

The fifth chapter of the Manusmṛti has now been cited extensively, and its position on meat-eating is clear: eating meat is generally forbidden, but ritual exceptions exist. Nevertheless, one verse from the chapter is often quoted in isolation to claim that meat is permitted in every context.

Show Sanskrit

न मांसभक्षणे दोषो न मद्ये न च मैथुने। प्रवृत्तिरेषा भूतानां निवृत्तिस्तु महाफला॥

“There is no fault in eating meat, drinking liquor, or engaging in intercourse; these are the natural tendencies of living beings, but abstention from them yields great rewards.”

—Manusmṛti 5.56

The intended meaning should now be clear from the rest of the chapter. The verse does not say that meat, liquor, and intercourse are permissible in every circumstance. It says that no universal fault attaches to them: they may be lawful in some contexts and prohibited in others. Meat and liquor are lawful in prescribed ritual contexts, while intercourse is lawful for procreation, as the Bhāgavata explains.

Medhātithi clarifies the point:

“It has been asserted that ‘there is no sin in the eating of meat.’ This means that there is no sin if one eats meat remaining from the worship of the gods, meat eaten at the wish of brāhmaṇas, or meat eaten under the other circumstances specified above—and only if he wishes to eat it. These three things, in their strictly restricted forms, constitute the natural way of living beings sanctioned by scripture for the maintenance of the body.”

The final phrase, however—“abstention yields great rewards”—brings us to the next point. Although scripture permits meat in certain exceptional cases, it does not encourage meat-eating; on the contrary, it highly praises abstention.

Show Sanskrit

वर्षे वर्षेऽश्वमेधेन यो यजेत शतं समाः। मांसानि च न खादेद्यस्तयोः पुण्यफलं समम्॥
फलमूलाशनैर्मेध्यैर्मुन्यन्नानां च भोजनैः। न तत्फलमवाप्नोति यन्मांसपरिवर्जनात्॥

“One who performs an Aśvamedha every year for a hundred years and one who abstains from meat receive equal fruits of merit. Even by eating pure fruits, roots, and the foods of sages, one does not attain as much merit as one gains by abstaining from meat.”

—Manusmṛti 5.53–54

The Mahābhārata also praises abstention from meat at length.

Show Sanskrit

सप्तर्षयो वालखिल्यास्तथैव च मरीचिपाः। मांसस्याभक्षणं राजन् प्रशंसन्ति मनीषिणः॥
न भक्षयति यो मांसं न च हन्यान्न घातयेत्। तन्मित्रं सर्वभूतानां मनुः स्वायम्भुवोऽब्रवीत्॥
अद्वेष्यः सर्वभूतानां विश्वस्यः सर्वजन्तुषु। साधूनां सम्मतो नित्यं भवेन्मांसं विवर्जयन्॥

“The seven ṛṣis, the Vālakhilyas, the Marīcipas, and other great sages praise abstention from meat, O king. He who neither eats meat, harms, nor causes harm is the friend of all beings, as Svāyambhuva Manu declared. By abstaining from meat, one becomes unhated by all beings, trusted by every creature, and always approved by the good.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116

Show Sanskrit

ददाति यजते चापि तपस्वी च भवत्यपि। मधुमांसनिवृत्त्येति प्राहैवं स बृहस्पतिः॥
मासि मास्यश्वमेधेन यो यजेत शतं समाः। न खादति च यो मांसं सममेतन्मतं मम॥
सदा यजति सत्रेण सदा दानं प्रयच्छति। सदा तपस्वी भवति मद्यमांसविवर्जनात्॥
सर्वे वेदा न तत्कुर्युः सर्वे यज्ञाश्च भारत। यो भक्षयित्वा मांसानि पश्चादपि निवर्तते॥

“By abstaining from meat and honey, one at once gives charity, performs Vedic ritual, and becomes an ascetic; so Bṛhaspati declared. One who performs an Aśvamedha every month for a hundred years and one who abstains from meat are, in my view, equal. By abstaining from wine and meat, one perpetually performs great Vedic sacrifices, practises charity, and undertakes austerity. All the Vedas and all rituals together, O descendant of Bharata, cannot produce the merit gained by one who gives up meat even after having eaten it in the past.”

Scripture praises not only complete abstinence, but also gradual abstinence through fortnightly, monthly, or yearly vows.

Show Sanskrit

कौमुद्यास्तु विशेषेण शुक्लपक्षे नराधिप। वर्जयेत्सर्वमांसानि धर्मो ह्यत्र विधीयते॥
अथवा मासमेकं वै सर्वमांसान्यभक्षयन्। अतीत्य सर्वदुःखानि सुखं जीवेद् निरामयः॥
वर्जयन्ति हि मांसानि मासशः पक्षशोऽपि वा। तेषां हिंसानिवृत्तानां ब्रह्मलोको विधीयते॥
अमांसभक्षणं चैव कौमुद्यां पार्थ राजभिः। सर्वभूतात्मभूतैस्तैर्विदितार्थपरावरैः॥

“One should especially abstain from all meat during the bright fortnight, O king; this is the prescribed dharma. Alternatively, one may abstain from all meat for an entire month; thereby, having passed beyond all sorrow, one lives happily and free from illness. Those who abstain from meat for a month or even a fortnight, having withdrawn from violence, are destined for Brahmaloka. Kings of old, O son of Pṛthā—men who regarded themselves as one with all beings and knew the higher and lower truths—abstained from meat during the moonlit season.”

Show Sanskrit

नात्मनोऽस्ति प्रियतरः पृथिव्यामनुसृत्य ह। तस्मात्प्राणिषु सर्वेषु दयावानात्मवान् भवेत्॥
सर्वमांसानि यो राजन् यावज्जीवं न भक्षयेत्। स्वर्गे स विपुलं स्थानं प्राप्नुयान्नात्र संशयः॥
धन्यं यशस्यमायुष्यं स्वर्ग्यं स्वस्त्ययनं महत्। मांसस्याभक्षणं प्राहुर्नियताः परमर्षयः॥

“Nothing on earth is dearer to a being than its own self. Therefore, one should be compassionate towards every living being, regarding them as oneself. He who abstains from all meat throughout his life, O king, attains an exalted place in heaven; of this there is no doubt. The great ṛṣis declare abstention from meat to bring prosperity, fame, long life, heaven, and great welfare.”

Guṇa-classification of meat

Having addressed a pramāṇa used to justify all meat, let us now address one used to condemn every form of meat-eating.

Objection. The Mahābhārata and Skanda Purāṇa recount a story in which ṛṣis condemn an animal sacrifice being performed by Indra.

Show Sanskrit

ततो दीनान् पशून् दृष्ट्वा ऋषयस्ते तपोधनाः। ऊचुः शक्रं समागम्य नायं यज्ञविधिः शुभः॥
अपविज्ञानमेतत्ते महान्तं धर्ममिच्छतः। न हि यज्ञे पशुगणा विधिदृष्टाः पुरंदर॥
धर्मोपघातकस्त्वेष समारम्भस्तव प्रभो। नायं धर्मकृतो धर्मो न हिंसा धर्म उच्यते॥

“Seeing the miserable animals bound for sacrifice, the ascetic ṛṣis approached Indra and said: ‘This is not an auspicious procedure of yajña. Although you seek dharma, this is a grave error in judgement. O Indra, animal sacrifices are not prescribed in this yajña. What you have begun violates dharma; an act does not become dharma merely because it is performed in its name, and violence is not called dharma.’”

—Mahābhārata 14.94.12–14

The same story appears in the Skanda Purāṇa 2.9.6:

Show Sanskrit

महर्षय उपाजग्मुस्तत्र भास्करवर्चसः। सम्मानिताः सुरगणैः पाद्यार्घ्यस्वागतादिभिः॥
ते बृहन्मुनयोऽपश्यन्मेध्यांस्तान् क्रोशतः पशून्। धर्मव्यतिक्रमं दृष्ट्वा कृपया ते द्विजोत्तमाः॥
महेन्द्रप्रमुखानूचुर्देवान् धर्मधियस्ततः। अहिंसैव परो धर्मस्तत्र वेदेऽस्ति कीर्तितः। साक्षात्पशुवधो यज्ञे न हि वेदस्य सम्मतः॥

“The great radiant ṛṣis arrived at the yajña and were honoured by the deities with the washing of their feet, offerings, and words of welcome. They then saw the sacrificial animals crying out. Moved by compassion at this violation of dharma, those best of brāhmaṇas spoke to the deities, with Indra at their head: ‘Ahiṃsā is the highest dharma, as the Veda declares. The actual killing of animals in this yajña is not approved by the Veda.’”

Answer. Read in full, the story does not condemn animal sacrifice absolutely. It introduces a classification by guṇa: devotees of Viṣṇu, including the devas, should not perform it, although it remains permitted for Śaivas and others. The ṛṣis do not say that animals are never sacrificed; they say that this particular sacrifice does not accord with the applicable vidhi. Beginning with verse 18 of the Skanda Purāṇa version:

Show Sanskrit

यादृशो हि गुणो यस्य स्वभावस्तस्य तादृशः। स्वस्वभावानुसारेण प्रवृत्तिः स्याच्च मानवैः॥ १८॥
सात्त्विकानां हि वो देवः साक्षाद्विष्णू रमापतिः। अहिंसायज्ञेऽस्ति ततोऽधिकारस्तस्य तुष्टये॥ १९॥
प्रत्यक्षपशुमालभ्य यज्ञस्याचरणं तु यत्। धर्मः स विपरीतो वै युष्माकं सुरसत्तमाः॥

“As one’s guṇa is, so is one’s nature; and according to one’s nature, one inclines towards particular actions. For sāttvika beings such as you devas, Viṣṇu, the husband of Sītā, is the deity; you are therefore qualified to please him through an ahiṃsā-yajña. The practice of sacrificing an actual animal is contrary to dharma for you devas.”

The ṛṣis then clarify that animal sacrifice is permitted for non-Vaiṣṇavas:

Show Sanskrit

राजसानां तामसानामासुराणां तथा नृणाम्। यथागुणं भैरवाद्या उपास्याः सन्ति देवताः॥ २३॥
स्वगुणानुगुणात्मीयदेवतातुष्टये भुवि। हिंस्रयज्ञविधानं यत्तेषामेवोचितं हि तत्॥ २४॥
तत्रापि विष्णुभक्ता ये दैत्यरक्षोनरादयः। तेषामप्युचितो नास्ति हिंसायज्ञः कुतस्तु वः॥ २५॥

“Among rājasic and tāmasic people, whether demons or humans, deities such as Bhairava are worshipped according to their guṇas. For pleasing a deity corresponding to their guṇa, a hiṃsā-yajña involving animal sacrifice is appropriate. Yet even among demons, rākṣasas, and humans, such a sacrifice is not appropriate for devotees of Viṣṇu—how much less for you devas.”

॥ इति मांसनिर्णयचन्द्रिकायां पूर्वार्धं सम्पूर्णम् ॥

3. Uttarārdha

3.1 Cow and other forbidden meats

Hunting and prescribed ritual contexts are valid exceptions for eating meat, but this does not mean that every animal may be sacrificed and eaten. As seen in the Devī Bhāgavatam, goats, buffaloes, and wild boars are preferred for sacrifice. The Manusmṛti 5.12–18 elaborates on lawful and forbidden meats.

Show Sanskrit

क्रव्यादान् शकुनान् सर्वांस्तथा ग्रामनिवासिनः। अनिर्दिष्टांश्चैकशफान् टिट्टिभं च विवर्जयेत्॥
कलविङ्कं प्लवं हंसं चक्राह्वं ग्रामकुक्कुटम्। सारसं रज्जुवालं च दात्यूहं शुकसारिके॥
मत्स्यादान् विड्वराहांश्च मत्स्यानेव च सर्वशः॥
पाठीनरोहितावाद्यौ नियुक्तौ हव्यकव्ययोः। राजीवान् सिंहतुण्डांश्च सशल्कांश्चैव सर्वशः॥

“He shall avoid all carnivorous birds and all birds that live in villages, unspecified one-hoofed animals, and the ṭiṭṭibha; the sparrow, plava, haṃsa, cakravāka, domesticated chicken, crane, rajjuvāla, dātyūha, parrot, and starling; fish-eating animals, the domesticated pig, and all fish—except the pāṭhīna and rohita when properly dedicated in rites, as well as the rājīva, siṃhatuṇḍa, and all fish with scales.”

Among birds, carnivorous species such as herons and vultures, along with village-dwelling species such as pigeons and crows, are forbidden. One-hoofed animals such as horses, donkeys, and mules are also forbidden, as are several specifically named birds, including the chicken—perhaps the most widely eaten meat today. Fish and fish-eating animals are forbidden, with exceptions for the listed species when properly consecrated.

Show Sanskrit

न भक्षयेदेकचरानज्ञातांश्च मृगद्विजान्। भक्ष्येष्वपि समुद्दिष्टान् सर्वान् पञ्चनखांस्तथा॥
श्वाविधं शल्यकं गोधां खड्गकूर्मशशांस्तथा। भक्ष्यान् पञ्चनखेष्वाहुरनुष्ट्रांश्चैकतोदतः॥

“He shall not eat solitary or unknown animals and birds, even when they resemble species declared fit for consumption; nor shall he eat five-nailed animals. Among five-nailed animals, the porcupine, hedgehog, alligator, rhinoceros, tortoise, and hare are declared fit to eat, as are animals having one row of teeth—with the exception of the camel.”

Also forbidden are animals that do not live in herds, such as owls and snakes, and newly discovered or unknown species even when they resemble permitted animals. Five-nailed animals such as monkeys are forbidden, subject to the stated exceptions. Animals with one row of teeth are generally allowed, except for the camel. The broad principle is that domesticated animals (grāmya) and carnivorous animals are forbidden.

These verses also demonstrate how Manu and other śāstras operate through the utsarga–apavāda system. Manu first says that all fish are forbidden—the general rule—and immediately allows five species as exceptions. Likewise, he first prohibits all five-nailed animals and then names exceptions. The text does not need to say explicitly, “These are exceptions”; the relationship is understood.

Beef

The most famous prohibition in Hinduism, however, is the prohibition on beef. Many misconceptions—whether genuine or maliciously spread—claim that beef was not originally forbidden in Hinduism and that the prohibition is a later development. The scriptures tell a different story.

Show Sanskrit

स धेन्वनडुहौ नाश्नीयाद्धेन्वनडुहौ वा इदं सर्वं बिभृतस्ते।

“Let him not eat the flesh of either the cow or the ox, for the cow and the ox support everything here on earth.”

—Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 3.1.2.21

Show Sanskrit

राजहा ब्रह्महा गोघ्नश्चोरः प्राणिवधे रतः। नास्तिकः परिवेत्ता च सर्वे निरयगामिनः॥

“The regicide, the killer of a brāhmaṇa, the killer of a cow, the thief, one who delights in killing living beings, the denier of the Vedas, and the transgressor—all go to hell.”

—Rāmāyaṇa 1.17.36

Show Sanskrit

अघ्न्या इति गवां नाम क एनान् हन्तुमर्हति। महच्चकाराकुशलं पृषध्रो गामभिन्निव॥
ऋषयो यतयो ह्येतन्नहुषे प्रत्यवेदयन्। गां मातरं चाप्यवधीर्वृषभं च प्रजापतिम्॥

“Cows are called aghnya—never to be killed. Who would kill them? Pṛṣadhra committed a great wrong by striking the cow as though she were a wild animal. The ascetic ṛṣis therefore said to Nahuṣa: ‘You have killed the cow, who is the mother, and the bull, who is Prajāpati.’”

—Mahābhārata 12.254.45–46

Show Sanskrit

आचार्यं च प्रवक्तारं पितरं मातरं गुरुम्। न हिंस्याद् ब्राह्मणान् गाश्च सर्वांश्चैव तपस्विनः॥

“One should never harm the teacher, the expounder, one’s father, mother, or preceptor, brāhmaṇas, cows, or any ascetic.”

—Manusmṛti 4.162

Objection. These verses prohibit hiṃsā, but do not explicitly prohibit eating.

Answer. Eating, selling, approving, and the other acts involved are all treated as forms of killing.

Show Sanskrit

अनुमन्ता विशसिता निहन्ता क्रयविक्रयी। संस्कर्ता चोपहर्ता च खादकश्चेति घातकाः॥

“He who approves, he who cuts up, he who kills, he who buys or sells, he who cooks, he who serves, and he who eats are all slayers.”

—Manusmṛti 5.51

Show Sanskrit

धनेन क्रयिको हन्ति खादकश्चोपभोगतः। घातको वधबन्धाभ्यामित्येष त्रिविधो वधः॥

“The buyer kills through his money, the eater through consumption, and the slaughterer through binding and killing the animal. These are the three forms of killing.”

—Mahābhārata 13.116

Show Sanskrit

भ्रूणहा पुरहन्ता च गोघ्नश्च मुनिसत्तम। यान्ति ते नरकं रोधं यश्चोच्छ्वासनिरोधकः॥

—Agni Purāṇa 227.32

Show Sanskrit

राजा गवाभिचाराद्यं हन्याच्चैवाततायिनः।

“The king should execute the killer of a cow, as he should other violent aggressors.”

—Agni Purāṇa 227.40

Show Sanskrit

पादमुद्यम्य यो विप्रं हन्ति गां पितरौ गुरुम्। रौरवे नियतो वासस्तस्य नास्तीह निष्कृतिः॥

“One who raises his foot against a brāhmaṇa or kills a cow, a parent, or a guru dwells in Raurava for an indefinite time; there is no expiation for him here.”

—Padma Purāṇa 1.48.18

Show Sanskrit

गवाशिनो दुरात्मानो ह्यभक्ष्यभक्षणे रताः। कीटयोनिं व्रजन्त्येते तरवश्च पिपीलिकाः॥

“Evil people who eat the flesh of cows and delight in consuming what is forbidden are born in their next lives as insects, trees, or ants.”

—Padma Purāṇa 1.76.17

These are among many other Purāṇic references.

Objection. The Vedas contain provisions for the sacrifice of cows in rites such as the Gomedha.

Answer. Such provisions exist, but they are no longer permitted in Kali-yuga.

Show Sanskrit

अश्वमेधं गवालम्भं संन्यासं पलपैतृकम्। देवरेण सुतोत्पत्तिं कलौ पञ्च विवर्जयेत्॥

“The horse sacrifice, the cow sacrifice, the renunciate order, certain ancestral rites, and the begetting of a son by one’s brother-in-law—these five are forbidden in Kali-yuga.”

—Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa 4.115.112

The Mahābhārata similarly states:

Show Sanskrit

पशुत्वाच्च विनिर्मुक्ताः प्रदानायोपकल्पिताः। ता इमा विप्रमुख्येभ्यो यो ददाति महीपते॥

“Cows are exempt from the category of sacrificial animals and are ordained only for gifting. One should give them to worthy brāhmaṇas, O king.”

—Mahābhārata 12.65.42–43

3.2 Deśācāra and health: valid exceptions?

Two other cases are sometimes claimed as exceptions to the prohibition on meat. Deśācāra is recognised by a few ancient ācāryas, while health reasons are cited mostly in modern times. Let us examine both.

Deśācāra: disputed

Some ācāryas from regions with ancient customs of meat-eating have argued that the inhabitants of those regions may continue such practices even outside sacrificial contexts. They cite the following passage from the Bṛhaspati Smṛti 1.26–30 and Śukranīti 4.46–49:

Show Sanskrit

देशजातिकुलादीनां ये धर्मास्तत्प्रवर्तिताः। तथैव ते पालनीयाः प्रजा प्रक्षुभ्यतेऽन्यथा॥ जनापरक्तिर्भवति बलं कोशश्च नश्यति। उदूह्यते दाक्षिणात्यैर्मातुलस्य सुता द्विजैः…

“Whatever customs pertain to a region, caste, or clan are to be tolerated; otherwise, the populace rebels, its loyalty is lost, and the army and treasury are destroyed. Twice-born men of the south marry the daughters of their maternal uncles…”

The argument is that practices followed in particular regions (deśa), castes (jāti), or clans (kula) should be permitted—not because they are inherently moral, but as a pragmatic measure to keep the populace in order. The passage then lists local practices.

The relevant statement for our purposes is:

Show Sanskrit

मत्स्यादाश्च नराः पूर्वे।

“People in the east eat fish.”

Some ācāryas from eastern regions such as Bengal, including Raghunandana, have used this statement to argue that eastern people may eat fish even when it has not been ritually consecrated.

Because this position was accepted by respected ācāryas and has scriptural support, it is not invalid in itself. Nevertheless, the great majority of other ācāryas rejected it. The Gautama Dharmasūtra 9.20, for example, states:

Show Sanskrit

देशजातिकुलधर्माश्चाम्नायैरविरुद्धाः प्रमाणम्॥ २०॥

“The customs of a region, caste, and clan may be taken as a basis of dharma if they do not contradict the Vedic scriptures.”

The majority view is therefore that local customs may be tolerated only when they do not contradict the Vedic scriptures. Because scripture strongly condemns meat-eating outside ritual contexts or hunting, such customs are to be rejected. The statement of the Bṛhaspati Smṛti and Śukranīti may instead be understood as a pragmatic rule of nīti: the king tolerates these practices lest the populace rebel, rather than declaring the practices themselves to be dharmic.

Even the ācāryas who recognise deśācāra as an exception for meat-eating restrict it to ancient local customs, not new practices. One therefore cannot argue, for example, that a Malayali may eat beef merely because the practice has recently become prevalent in Kerala.

Health reasons: entirely condemned

Another justification commonly offered, especially in modern times, is health.

First, this work does not deny the nutritional benefits of meat. Some vegetarians approach the issue by attempting to deny meat’s nutritional superiority, but its nutritive value is acknowledged in scripture, Ayurveda, and modern science. Because this is a scriptural analysis, only the scriptural position will be stated here.

Show Sanskrit

न मांसात्परमं किंचिद्रसतो विद्यते भुवि॥
क्षतक्षीणाभितप्तानां ग्राम्यधर्मरतात्मनाम्। अध्वना कर्शितानां च न मांसाद्विद्यते परम्॥
सद्यो वर्धयति प्राणान् पुष्टिमग्र्यां दधाति च॥

“There is nothing on earth superior to meat in taste. Nothing is superior to meat for the injured, emaciated, exhausted, those engaged in labour, or those wearied by travel. It quickly increases vitality and provides excellent nourishment.”

—Mahābhārata 13.117.7–8

Although meat’s power to produce nourishment (vṛddhi) is recognised, seeking that nourishment is condemned in the same chapter.

Show Sanskrit

स्वमांसं परमांसेन यो वर्धयितुमिच्छति। नास्ति क्षुद्रतरस्तस्मान्नृशंसतरो नरः॥
न हि प्राणात्प्रियतरं लोके किंचन विद्यते। तस्माद्दयां नरः कुर्याद्यथात्मनि तथा परे॥

“He who wishes to nourish his own flesh with the flesh of another—there is no man lower or more cruel than he. Nothing in the world is dearer than life; therefore, one should show the same compassion to others as to oneself.”

—Mahābhārata 13.117.13

The formula स्वमांसं परमांसेन यो वर्धयितुमिच्छति—“He who wishes to nourish his own flesh with the flesh of another”—recurs throughout scripture with many condemnations.

Show Sanskrit

स्वमांसं परमांसेन यो वर्धयितुमिच्छति। अनभ्यर्च्य पितॄन् देवांस्ततोऽन्यो नास्त्यपुण्यकृत्॥

“He who wishes to nourish his own flesh with the flesh of another, except during rites for the deities and ancestors—there is no one more sinful than he.”

—Manusmṛti 5.52 and Viṣṇu Smṛti 51.74

Show Sanskrit

स्वमांसं परमांसेन यो वर्धयति निर्दयः। स नूनं नरकं याति प्रोक्तमेतन्महर्षिभिः॥

“The pitiless person who nourishes his own flesh with the flesh of another certainly goes to hell; so the great ṛṣis have declared.”

—Skanda Purāṇa 6.22.2

Show Sanskrit

स्वमांसं परमांसेन यो वर्धयितुमिच्छति। न पापकृत्तमो लोके तस्मादन्यो हि विद्यते॥
असंस्कृतान् पशून् मन्त्रैर्नाद्याद्विप्रः कथंचन। संस्कृतानद्याच्छाश्वतं विधिमास्थितः॥

“There is no greater sinner in the world than one who wishes to nourish his own flesh with the flesh of another. A brāhmaṇa should never eat an animal that has not been consecrated with mantras; he may eat one that has been consecrated, while remaining established in the eternal rule.”

—Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa 3.25.24

Thus, although the nutritional value of meat is recognised, one should not eat it for that reason, because doing so amounts to nourishing one’s own flesh with the flesh of another.

3.3 Did Lord Rāma eat meat?

We now come to the final topic, which is perhaps the most controversial apart from beef.

This entire paper should make clear that it would not be morally problematic if Rāma ate meat. Many Hindus—especially vegetarian devotees of Lord Rāma—regard the claim as an accusation and deny it for that reason. Yet, as shown by the passages from the Manusmṛti, Mahābhārata, and Bhāgavata, eating hunted meat is entirely lawful for a kṣatriya such as Rāma. Rāma himself says:

Show Sanskrit

मांसहेतोरपि मृगान् विनोदार्थं च धन्विनः। घ्नन्ति लक्ष्मण राजानो मृगयायां महावने॥

“O Lakṣmaṇa, kings and bowmen hunt wild animals in the great forest both for sport and for meat.”

—Rāmāyaṇa 3.43.30

We may now examine passages from Vālmīki’s Rāmāyaṇa—all within the context of sacrifice or hunting—in which Rāma ate meat.

Rāma first commands Lakṣmaṇa to hunt and bring him meat:

Show Sanskrit

मृगं हत्वानय क्षिप्रं लक्ष्मणेह शुभेक्षण। कर्तव्यः शास्त्रदृष्टो हि विधिर्धर्ममनुस्मर॥
भ्रातुर्वचनमाज्ञाय लक्ष्मणः परवीरहा। चकार स यथोक्तं च तं रामः पुनरब्रवीत्॥
ऐणेयं श्रपयस्वैतच्छालां यक्ष्यामहे वयम्। त्वर सौम्य मुहूर्तोऽयं ध्रुवश्च दिवसोऽप्ययम्॥
स लक्ष्मणः कृष्णमृगं मेध्यं हत्वा प्रतापवान्। अथ चिक्षेप सौमित्रिः समिद्धे जातवेदसि॥
तं तु पक्वं परिज्ञाय निष्टप्तं छिन्नशोणितम्। लक्ष्मणः पुरुषव्याघ्रमथ राघवमब्रवीत्॥
अयं सर्वः समस्ताङ्गः श्रुतः कृष्णमृगो मया। देवतां देवसंकाश यजस्व कुशलो ह्यसि॥
रामः स्नात्वा तु नियतो गुणवान् जप्यकोविदः। संग्रहेणाकरोत्सर्वान् मन्त्रान् सत्रावसानिकान्॥

Rāma said: “O Lakṣmaṇa, quickly kill a deer and bring it here. Remember dharma, for the prescribed rite is to be performed according to scripture.” Having received his brother’s command, Lakṣmaṇa, the slayer of enemy heroes, did as instructed. Rāma then said: “Cook this deer. We shall perform the sacrifice with it. Hurry, gentle one; the auspicious moment has begun, and the day is passing.” Lakṣmaṇa, son of Sumitrā, killed a consecrated black deer and placed it in the blazing fire. When he knew it to be fully cooked and drained of blood, he said to Rāma: “This black deer has been thoroughly cooked by me, complete in all its limbs. O you who resemble the gods, offer it to the deity, for you are skilled in the rite.” The disciplined and virtuous Rāma then bathed and, being versed in recitation, performed all the concluding mantras of the ritual.

—Rāmāyaṇa 2.56.23–28

Another passage in the Ayodhyākāṇḍa describes Rāma offering meat to Sītā:

Show Sanskrit

तां तथा दर्शयित्वा तु मैथिलीं गिरिनिम्नगाम्। निषसाद गिरिप्रस्थे सीतां मांसेन छन्दयन्॥
इदं मेध्यमिदं स्वादु निष्टप्तमिदमग्निना। एवमास्ते स धर्मात्मा सीतया सह राघवः॥

“Having shown the mountain river to the princess of Mithilā, Rāma sat upon the mountainside and gratified Sītā with meat, saying, ‘This is sacrificial; this is tasty; this has been well roasted over the fire.’ Thus the righteous descendant of Raghu sat there with Sītā.”

These are instances of eating sacrificial meat. The following passages concern hunting.

Show Sanskrit

तौ तत्र हत्वा चतुरो महामृगान् वराहमृश्यं पृषतं महारुरुम्। आदाय मेध्यं त्वरितं बुभुक्षितौ वासाय काले ययतुर्वनस्पतिम्॥

“There the two of them, Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa, being hungry, killed four large wild animals—a wild boar, a white-footed antelope, a spotted deer, and a great black deer. Quickly taking the consecrated meat, they went at evening to a tree under which to spend the night.”

—Rāmāyaṇa 2.52.102

The word bubhukṣitau, “hungry,” makes clear that the hunting was undertaken for food. The passage also uses medhyam. This word comes from medha, a Vedic ritual, and means “sacrificial” or “worthy of sacrifice.” Vālmīki thereby clarifies that, because of Agastya’s ancient sacrifice, wild animals are medhya and fit to be eaten.

The word is used again in another hunting passage:

Show Sanskrit

क्रोशमात्रं ततो गत्वा भ्रातरौ रामलक्ष्मणौ। बहून् मेध्यान् मृगान् हत्वा चेरतुर्यमुनावने॥

“After travelling only about a krośa, the brothers Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa killed many medhya animals and moved through the Yamunā forest.”

—Rāmāyaṇa 2.55.3

Show Sanskrit

निहत्य पृषतं चान्यं मांसमादाय राघवः। त्वरमाणो जनस्थानं ससाराभिमुखस्तदा॥

“Having killed another spotted deer and taken its meat, Rāma quickly proceeded towards Janasthāna.”

—Rāmāyaṇa 3.44.27

The sheer number of these passages should be sufficient to establish that Rāma ate meat, but only within the scripturally prescribed contexts of hunting and sacrifice. Several objections are nevertheless raised.

Objections

I. Objection: Māṃsa means the pulp of a fruit. This meaning appears in many dictionaries and is advanced by the commentator Śivasahāya.

Answer. Even in English, the word “meat” can mean the edible part of a nut or fruit. But when a passage states that someone hunted particular animals and ate the meat, the context makes clear that the meat belongs to the animals named in the same sentence, not to an unmentioned fruit.

In addition to the context of hunting or sacrifice, words such as bubhukṣitau (“hungry”) show that hunting was undertaken for food, while medhyam identifies the result as sacrificially fit.

Moreover, meat is prohibited in the first place because of ahiṃsā. To admit that Rāma hunted or sacrificed animals, but deny that he ate the meat, defeats the moral purpose of the argument: hunting purely for sport and wasting the animal would be worse than hunting and eating it. Sacrificial meat—including, in this framework, hunted meat—is havis or prasāda, and wasting it is sinful.

Finally, Śivasahāya is the only commentator who denies that Rāma ate meat. Govindarāja, Amṛtakaṭaka, Nāgeśabhaṭṭa, and the other commentators agree that he did. Śivasahāya is therefore an extreme outlier. Consider their explanations of Rāmāyaṇa 2.52.102:

Show Sanskrit

तौ तत्र हत्वा चतुरो महामृगान् वराहमृश्यं पृषतं महारुरुम्। आदाय मेध्यं त्वरितं बुभुक्षितौ वासाय काले ययतुर्वनस्पतिम्॥

Govindarāja:

Show Sanskrit

मेध्यमिति ऋश्यादिसर्वविशेषणम्।

“Medhyam qualifies the deer and the other animals.”

Show Sanskrit

बुभुक्षितौ तेषां मेध्यं मांसं त्वरितमादायेति। बुभुक्षावशात् केवलमग्नितापमात्रं कृत्वा स्वीकृत्येत्यर्थः। उक्ताहारस्य वन्याहारान्तर्गतत्वात् ग्रहः। “देवान् पितॄन् समभ्यर्च्य खादन् मांसं न दोषभाक्” इति शास्त्रात्।

“Being hungry, the two quickly took the medhya meat. Because of their hunger, they accepted it after only lightly roasting it over the fire. Deer meat is included among foods fit to be eaten in the forest, as scripture states: ‘One incurs no fault by eating meat after honouring the deities and ancestors.’”

—Amṛtakaṭaka:

Show Sanskrit

मांसस्य च वन्याहारान्तर्गतत्वान्न दोषः मृगयाधर्मकत्वाच्च न मृगहनने दोषः। बुभुक्षितौ हत्वा तानादाय गतावित्यनेन भोजनमर्थसिद्धमेवेति।

“Because deer meat is included within forest food, there is no fault in eating it. Nor is there fault in killing deer, since hunting is the dharma of kṣatriyas. The statement that, being hungry, they killed the animals and took them away necessarily implies that they ate them.”

—Nāgeśabhaṭṭa:

Show Sanskrit

वराहादीन् चतुरो महामृगान् हत्वा खेलनार्थं सन्ताड्य बुभुक्षितौ तौ रामलक्ष्मणौ मेध्यं व्रतिभिरभोक्तव्यं फलादिकमित्यर्थः।

“Having ‘killed’—that is, playfully struck—the four great animals beginning with the boar, the hungry Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa took medhyam, meaning fruits and similar foods fit to be eaten by those observing a vow.”

—Śivasahāya:

This comparison shows that Śivasahāya’s interpretations of hatvā as “playfully struck” and medhyam as “fruit” are unorthodox and contrary to the consensus of the commentators.

II. Objection: Rāma promised not to eat meat.

Show Sanskrit

चतुर्दश हि वर्षाणि वत्स्यामि विजने वने। मधुमूलफलैर्जीवन् हित्वा मुनिवदामिषम्॥

Rāma said: “I shall dwell in the secluded forest for fourteen years, living on honey, roots, and fruit, and giving up āmiṣa like a sage.”

—Rāmāyaṇa 2.20.29

Answer. Here āmiṣa does not mean meat, but the pleasures of royal life. Every commentator, including Śivasahāya—the strongest proponent of the view that Rāma did not eat meat—explains it this way.

Show Sanskrit

आमिषं राजभोग्यं वस्तु। “आमिषं पुनपुंसकम्। भोग्यवस्तुनि सम्भोगे” इति मेदिनी।

“Āmiṣa means an object of royal enjoyment, as stated in the Medinī dictionary.”

—Amṛtakaṭaka:

Show Sanskrit

आमिषं राजभोग्यं वस्तु हित्वा त्यक्त्वा। आकर्षणेऽपि रूढिः स्यादामिषम्।

“I shall give up āmiṣa, meaning objects of royal pleasure. Āmiṣa may also mean an attraction.”

—Śivasahāya:

Śivasahāya may have avoided taking āmiṣa to mean meat because such a reading would harm his own position. If Rāma promised to give up meat during exile alongside the other comforts he was renouncing, that would imply that he had previously eaten it.

Even if one interprets āmiṣa as meat, other commentators explain that it refers specifically to meat prepared by royal cooks.

Show Sanskrit

मुनिवदामिषं सूदैर्विशिष्टसंस्कारसंस्कृतं मांसम्। तेन “इदं मेध्यमिदं स्वादु निष्टप्तमिदमग्निना” इति वक्ष्यमाणेन न विरोधः।

“‘Giving up āmiṣa like a sage’ refers specifically to meat prepared and specially consecrated by royal cooks. It therefore does not contradict the later passage, ‘This is sacrificial; this is tasty; this is roasted over the fire.’”

—Nāgeśabhaṭṭa:

Show Sanskrit

अत्रामिषशब्देन सूदैः संस्कृतं मांसमुच्यते। केवलं मांसस्वीकारस्योत्तरत्र वक्ष्यमाणत्वात् “इदं मेध्यमिदं स्वादु निष्टप्तमिदमग्निना” इति।

“Here āmiṣa means meat prepared by royal cooks. It cannot mean meat in general, because the later passage says, ‘This is sacrificial; this is tasty; this is roasted over the fire.’”

—Govindarāja:

III. Objection: Hanumān says that Rāma does not eat meat.

Show Sanskrit

न मांसं राघवो भुङ्क्ते न चापि मधु सेवते। वन्यं सुविहितं नित्यं भक्तमश्नाति पञ्चमम्॥

“Rāma is not eating meat, nor is he drinking honey. He eats only what is available in the forest, and even that amounts to one fifth of his usual meal.”

—Rāmāyaṇa 5.36.41

Answer. This is not a description of Rāma’s normal habits. The word pañcamam—one fifth of a meal—already indicates an abnormal state, and the rest of the passage makes the reason explicit:

Show Sanskrit

न दंशान्न मशकान्न कीटान्न सरीसृपान्। राघवोऽपनयेद्गात्रात् त्वद्गतेनान्तरात्मना॥

“Rāma does not even drive flies, mosquitoes, insects, or reptiles from his body, because his heart is fixed upon you.”

Hanumān tells Sītā that Rāma is behaving this way out of grief at their separation. It is not his usual state—unless one supposes that he always ate only one fifth of a meal and habitually allowed mosquitoes and snakes to remain on his body.

Indeed, this strengthens the argument that Rāma did ordinarily eat meat, because Hanumān specifically mentions that he has ceased eating it as an expression of grief. Why mention abstention from meat alongside these other unusual acts if he had never eaten meat at all?

Show Sanskrit

दुष्यन्तेन करूशेन रामालर्कनलैस्तथा। विचकाश्वेन निमिना जनकेन च धीमता॥
एतैश्चान्यैश्च राजेन्द्र पुरा मांसं न भक्षितम्॥

“Duṣyanta, Karūśa, Rāma, Alarka, Nala, Vicakāśva, Nimi, the wise Janaka, and others, O king, did not eat meat in ancient times.”

—IV. Objection: Mahābhārata 13.116 lists Rāma among people who never ate meat.

Answer. Nothing in the passage indicates that the Rāma named here is Rāma, son of Daśaratha. Given the unanimous evidence of the Rāmāyaṇa itself, the verse may refer to another Rāma. The Mahābhārata mentions at least three figures of that name: Rāma, son of Jamadagni; Rāma, son of Vasudeva; and Rāma, son of Daśaratha. It was an extremely common name.

V. Objection: These passages are interpolations.

Answer. They are not: all these passages occur in the Critical Edition of the Rāmāyaṇa.
