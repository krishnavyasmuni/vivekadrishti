Mahabharata महाभारतम्

Mahabharata
महाभारतम्
TraditionItihasa / Sanskrit epic
Traditional authorVyasa
LanguageSanskrit
FormationLayered; chiefly later first millennium BCE to early centuries CE
Received structure18 parvas, often with the Harivamsha supplement
Traditional extentAbout 100,000 shlokas
Critical editionBORI project, 1919–1966

The Mahabharata follows the Pandavas and Kauravas from a disputed Kuru inheritance through the dice game, exile, failed diplomacy and the war at Kurukshetra. It is also a library of political argument, pilgrimage, myth, dharma, liberation teaching and devotion, all organized around the consequences of one family catastrophe.

This revision follows the epic’s own architecture. Each of the eighteen parvas receives a separate section, while date, recension and critical-edition history remain distinct from the plot summary.

Date and formation

The epic is the result of long oral, bardic, literary and scholastic transmission, not a work whose entire contents can be assigned to one year. Most modern histories place its main formation across the later first millennium BCE and the early centuries CE, while recognizing that individual stories may be older and recensional growth continued later.

The names Jaya, Bharata and Mahabharata, together with traditional totals of 8,800, 24,000 and 100,000 verses, record ancient ideas about expansion. They should not be turned into three securely recovered historical editions: the surviving manuscripts do not independently prove so simple a sequence.

Different blocks have different histories. The Kuru conflict, the Bhagavad Gita, pilgrimage material, the large Shanti and Anushasana collections and developed Narayana theology must be dated through language, metre, parallel passages, doctrine and manuscript distribution rather than by the age of the epic’s oldest narrative.

Structure and recensions

The received epic has eighteen parvas. Nested narrators—Ugrashravas Sauti, Vaishampayana and others—place the Kuru past inside later acts of recitation, including Janamejaya’s snake sacrifice. Many traditions attach the Harivamsha as a khila or supplement, but it has its own textual history.

Manuscripts form major northern and southern families with numerous regional subtraditions. “Eighteen parvas” therefore names a stable large-scale architecture, not identical wording or chapter numbering in every witness. The BORI edition reconstructs a common manuscript ancestor; regional passages remain essential to reception history even when excluded from its constituted text.

1. Adi Parva — Beginnings

The opening book establishes the recitation frames, Bharata and Bhrigu genealogies, Shantanu, Bhishma’s vow, the births of Dhritarashtra, Pandu and Vidura, and the childhood rivalry of their heirs. Training under Drona, the lacquer-house escape, Draupadi’s svayamvara and polyandrous marriage, and the founding of Indraprastha turn inherited family arrangements into the political conditions of the later war.

2. Sabha Parva — Assembly Hall

Maya builds the Pandava hall, Yudhishthira performs the Rajasuya and Shishupala is killed, but imperial success produces Duryodhana’s intensified resentment. Shakuni’s dice games strip Yudhishthira of kingdom, brothers, himself and Draupadi; her unanswered legal challenge in the assembly exposes institutional failure and sends the Pandavas into exile.

3. Vana Parva — Forest

Twelve years of exile become an encyclopaedic middle book of pilgrimage, debate, ascetic encounter and embedded narrative. Arjuna acquires divine weapons, while the stories of Nala and Damayanti, Savitri, and the Ramopakhyana mirror or answer the Pandavas’ losses; the Yaksha’s questions close the exile with a test of Yudhishthira’s judgment.

4. Virata Parva — Life in Disguise

During the thirteenth year the Pandavas conceal their identities at Virata’s court. Draupadi suffers harassment from Kichaka, Bhima kills him, and Arjuna finally reveals himself during the cattle raid; the book converts enforced anonymity into the recovery of public and martial identity.

5. Udyoga Parva — Preparations and Diplomacy

Both sides mobilize allies while embassies attempt to prevent war. Vidura’s counsel, the Sanatsujatiya and Krishna’s peace mission examine rule, self-command and the limits of compromise; Duryodhana’s refusal to yield even a minimal settlement makes the failure of diplomacy part of the epic’s assignment of responsibility.

6. Bhishma Parva — The War Begins

The armies assemble and the Bhagavad Gita answers Arjuna’s refusal to fight through teachings on self, action, knowledge, devotion and Krishna’s cosmic form. Bhishma commands the Kauravas for the first ten days and falls when Arjuna fights with Shikhandin before him, leaving Bhishma alive on his bed of arrows.

7. Drona Parva — Escalation

Drona assumes command and the conflict becomes more destructive. Abhimanyu is trapped and killed inside the battle formation, Arjuna vows to kill Jayadratha, Ghatotkacha’s night battle forces Karna to spend his divine weapon, and Drona is brought down after a deliberately ambiguous announcement concerning Ashvatthaman.

8. Karna Parva — Karna’s Command

Karna commands the Kaurava army as the war narrows toward his long-anticipated encounter with Arjuna. Shalya serves as his reluctant charioteer; curses and earlier choices converge when Karna’s chariot fails, and Arjuna kills him at Krishna’s urging.

9. Shalya Parva — The Eighteenth Day

Shalya becomes the final commander and is killed by Yudhishthira as the remaining Kaurava forces collapse. Balarama’s pilgrimage is interwoven with the closing battle, and Bhima defeats Duryodhana by striking his thigh in the mace duel, ending organized resistance through another contested act.

10. Sauptika Parva — Night Massacre

Ashvatthaman, Kripa and Kritavarman attack the sleeping Pandava camp and kill the remaining warriors, including Draupadi’s sons. The revenge extends violence beyond the formal battlefield; Ashvatthaman’s final weapon and its redirection toward the unborn Parikshit show that victory has not stopped the logic of annihilation.

11. Stri Parva — The Women

Gandhari, Kunti, Draupadi and the women of both houses lament among the dead while funeral rites begin. Gandhari blames Krishna for permitting destruction and curses the Yadavas; the book makes bereavement, not conquest, the immediate moral account of war.

12. Shanti Parva — Peace and Instruction

Yudhishthira wishes to renounce the throne, and Bhishma instructs him from the bed of arrows. Rajadharma treats government, punishment and counsel; Apaddharma considers action in crisis; Mokshadharma gathers wide-ranging teachings on self, renunciation, Samkhya-like analysis, Yoga and liberation.

13. Anushasana Parva — Precepts

Bhishma continues with teachings on conduct, gifts, vows, duties and religious praise. The book is highly composite and deeply connected with later dharma and dana traditions; after completing his instruction, Bhishma chooses the time of his death.

14. Ashvamedhika Parva — Horse Sacrifice

Yudhishthira’s ashvamedha ritually re-establishes sovereignty while Arjuna follows the horse through a landscape still marked by war. The Anugita revisits philosophical teaching after Arjuna says he has forgotten Krishna’s battlefield discourse, and the sacrifice closes without erasing the epic’s grief.

15. Ashramavasika Parva — Life in the Hermitage

Dhritarashtra, Gandhari and Kunti withdraw from the court to the forest, where the survivors meet the dead in a visionary reconciliation. The three elders later die in a forest fire, and Vidura’s death and absorption into Yudhishthira deepen the book’s movement from dynastic politics toward renunciation.

16. Mausala Parva — The Clubs

Gandhari’s curse is fulfilled when the Yadavas destroy one another at Prabhasa with clubs grown from the fatal iron fragment. Balarama and Krishna depart, Dvaraka is lost and Arjuna finds his former power failing; the allies of the victors share the impermanence that consumed the Kuru world.

17. Mahaprasthanika Parva — The Great Journey

The Pandavas install Parikshit and renounce the kingdom, travelling north with Draupadi and a dog. Draupadi and four brothers fall one by one as Yudhishthira names their failings, but he continues without abandoning the faithful animal who has followed him.

18. Svargarohana Parva — Ascent to Heaven

The dog is revealed as Dharma after Yudhishthira refuses heaven at the price of betrayal. A final vision of apparent hell and the presence of former enemies in heaven tests ordinary moral expectation before the heroes are restored to their divine forms and the recitation closes.

Harivamsha — Supplement

The Harivamsha is traditionally attached to the Mahabharata as a khila. It supplies creation and dynastic material and an extended life of Krishna, especially his birth, youth and Yadava setting, thereby connecting the epic with developing Puranic Krishna traditions; it should be cited through its own recension or critical edition.

Theology, influence and practice

Theology and dharma

Dharma is tested through conflicts among kinship, kingship, vow, justice and consequence rather than presented as one effortless code. Krishna’s Bhagavad Gita and Narayana traditions give the epic a powerful Vaishnava centre, while Shiva, the Goddess, sacrifice, asceticism and multiple paths to liberation preserve a deliberately plural religious world.

Influence and reception

The epic became a transregional archive for Sanskrit commentary, vernacular retelling, temple narrative, theatre, dance and modern literature. The Bhagavad Gita, Nala–Damayanti, Savitri, Shakuntala and many other units acquired independent lives, while different communities made Draupadi, Karna, Bhima or Krishna the centre of their Mahabharata.

Rites and historical practice

Its discussions of kingship, pilgrimage, sacrifice, gifts, marriage, social classification and renunciation are major sources for the history of normative thought. Because the books are layered and often stage disagreement, such passages must not be treated as direct statistics for one society or one date.

Critical edition

The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute’s project ran from 1919 to 1966 and consulted 1,259 manuscripts. Its editors reconstructed readings ancestral to the major surviving traditions and preserved excluded or variant material in a large apparatus and appendices; the result is not a claim to reproduce Vyasa’s autograph.

The constituted text and the regional recensions answer different historical questions. A passage may be secondary to the recoverable common ancestor yet ancient, influential and authoritative within a community; responsible use therefore reads the apparatus and reception history alongside the edited text.

Further reading

Begin with V. S. Sukthankar’s Prolegomena and the BORI critical edition for textual method; then use John Brockington for epic formation, J. A. B. van Buitenen or Bibek Debroy for English translation, and parva-specific scholarship for the didactic and theological corpora.
