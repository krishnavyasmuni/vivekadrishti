TraditionItihāsa / Sanskrit epic
Traditional authorVyāsa
LanguageSanskrit

Traditional extentAbout 100,000 ślokas

The Mahābhārata turns a disputed Kuru succession into an enormous inquiry into kinship, sovereignty, violence, dharma and liberation. The conflict between Pāṇḍavas and Kauravas moves through childhood rivalry, the creation of Indraprastha, imperial consecration, dice and dispossession, twelve years of forest exile, a failed peace, eighteen days of war and a postwar world in which victory itself becomes a moral and political burden.

The war story is only the epic’s spine. Around it are nested recitations, pilgrimage catalogues, law and kingship, the Bhagavad Gītā, the Nala and Sāvitrī stories, the Sanatsujātīya, the Nārāyaṇīya, mokṣa teaching, funeral lament and dynastic myth. These blocks have different histories; the received eighteen-parva Mahābhārata is therefore both a narrative and a deliberately encyclopedic corpus.

Date and formation

The Mahābhārata cannot be assigned to one composition date. Most modern histories place the main formation of the Sanskrit epic from the later first millennium BCE into roughly the third or fourth century CE, while recognizing both older heroic and genealogical traditions and later recensional development. Individual passages must therefore be dated by language, metre, intertextual dependence, doctrine and manuscript distribution rather than by the presumed antiquity of the Kuru war story.

The traditional names Jaya, Bhārata and Mahābhārata and associated totals of 8,800, 24,000 and 100,000 verses preserve an ancient theory of expansion. They are important evidence for how transmitters imagined the epic’s growth, but the extant manuscript tradition does not provide three recoverable editions that can simply be placed in chronological succession.

Different blocks clearly belong to different literary environments. The central dynastic conflict, the Bhagavad Gītā, the enormous Śānti and Anuśāsana didactic corpora, pilgrimage sections and developed Nārāyaṇa/Kṛṣṇa theology cannot all be dated by one argument. The epic’s history is the history of their combination into a work that presents itself as containing the full range of human aims and knowledge.

Structure and recensions

The received work has eighteen parvas. Its outer narrative begins with Ugraśravas Sauti speaking at Naimiṣa and encloses Vaiśaṃpāyana’s recitation to Janamejaya at the snake sacrifice; within that performance characters repeatedly tell still older narratives. This nested architecture is what allows genealogy, moral exempla, myth, pilgrimage and philosophy to enter the Kuru story without being mere appendices.

The manuscript tradition contains major northern and southern families and many regional subgroups. Parva order is stable at the largest scale, but chapter numbering, verse totals and the presence of long passages differ greatly. The Harivaṃśa is commonly transmitted as a khila, supplement, to the Mahābhārata but has its own manuscript and compositional history.

The BORI critical edition sought readings reconstructable from the broad manuscript tradition rather than reproducing one northern or southern vulgate. Its constituted text is therefore substantially shorter than many popular editions. Passages excluded from the constituted text remain essential evidence for regional transmission and reception.

1. Ādi Parva — origins of the catastrophe

The opening establishes the recitation frames, serpent-sacrifice background, Bhr̥gu and Bharata genealogies and the Kuru succession. Bhīṣma’s vow, Satyavatī’s dynastic strategy, niyoga, Pāṇḍu’s curse and the extraordinary births of Kauravas and Pāṇḍavas make legitimacy unstable before the cousins act. Droṇa’s school, Ekalavya, Karṇa’s appearance, the lacquer-house escape, Draupadī’s svayaṃvara and polyandrous marriage, the foundation of Indraprastha and the Khāṇḍava fire create the alliances, injuries and political claims from which the later war grows.

2. Sabhā Parva — imperial success and dispossession

Maya’s hall materializes Pāṇḍava sovereignty; Jarāsandha’s death and the rājasūya extend Yudhiṣṭhira’s political reach. The same hall then becomes the site of reversal when Śakuni’s dice strip Yudhiṣṭhira of wealth, kingdom, brothers, himself and Draupadī. Draupadī’s question—whether an already enslaved man could still stake his wife—exposes not a technical puzzle alone but the incapacity of the royal assembly to enforce the dharma it claims to embody.

3. Vana Parva — exile as an encyclopedic school

Twelve years in the forest become far more than waiting. Arjuna acquires divine weapons; pilgrimages map sacred India; Nala and Damayantī, Sāvitrī, Rāma and other embedded narratives offer analogues to loss, endurance and recovery; philosophical and ascetic dialogues repeatedly reinterpret the Pāṇḍavas’ disaster. The Yakṣa’s questions near the end turn Yudhiṣṭhira’s survival into a test of judgment rather than strength.

4. Virāṭa Parva — concealment and restored identity

The Pāṇḍavas spend the thirteenth year in disguised service at Virāṭa’s court. Draupadī’s harassment by Kīcaka and Bhīma’s retaliation expose the fragility of protection under concealment. The final cattle raid forces Arjuna to recover his weapons and public warrior identity; the book ends with the incognito condition fulfilled and a new Matsya alliance available for the coming war.

5. Udyoga Parva — diplomacy before war

Both sides recruit allies while repeated embassies test whether any settlement is still possible. Vidura’s counsel, the Sanatsujātīya and Kṛṣṇa’s mission place self-command, kingship and moral responsibility beside strategy. Duryodhana’s refusal to concede even the minimum territory sought by the Pāṇḍavas gives the failure of diplomacy a long argumentative history before the first weapon is used.

6. Bhīṣma Parva — Bhagavad Gītā and the opening war

Saṃjaya narrates the battlefield to Dhṛtarāṣṭra. Arjuna’s refusal to fight produces the Bhagavad Gītā, in which Kṛṣṇa coordinates self, action, knowledge, devotion and divine manifestation without removing the immediate problem of killing kin. Bhīṣma commands the Kaurava army for ten days and falls before Arjuna with Śikhaṇḍin positioned against him, leaving the oldest Kuru statesman alive on the bed of arrows.

7. Droṇa Parva — escalation and broken limits

Under Droṇa the war increasingly defeats its own warrior codes. Abhimanyu is isolated and killed inside the cakravyūha; Arjuna’s vow leads to Jayadratha’s death; the night battle forces Karṇa to expend Indra’s weapon against Ghaṭotkaca. Droṇa finally lays down his arms after the deliberately ambiguous announcement that “Aśvatthāman is dead,” binding victory to deception whose consequences continue after the war.

8. Karṇa Parva — rivalry reaches its end

Karṇa takes command with Śalya as a hostile charioteer. Earlier curses, gifts and choices converge in his duel with Arjuna: the loss of his divine armor and weapon, the chariot-wheel crisis and Kṛṣṇa’s insistence that Arjuna remember Abhimanyu and Draupadī make Karṇa’s death the culmination of a biography built from generosity, exclusion, loyalty and destructive allegiance.

9. Śalya Parva — collapse of the Kaurava army

Śalya becomes the final commander and is killed by Yudhiṣṭhira. Balarāma’s pilgrimage is interwoven with the battle’s closing movement before he returns for the mace duel. Bhīma defeats Duryodhana by striking the thigh, a forbidden target, and the organized war ends through another act the victors themselves must explain.

10. Sauptika Parva — violence after the battle

Aśvatthāman, Kṛpa and Kṛtavarman attack the sleeping Pāṇḍava camp and kill its remaining warriors, including Draupadī’s sons. Aśvatthāman’s final weapon is then directed toward the Pāṇḍava line in the womb of Uttarā. The book destroys any clean boundary between “war” and “after war”: vengeance continues after armies have ceased to exist.

11. Strī Parva — grief judges victory

The women of the Kuru houses enter the battlefield, identify bodies and lament sons, husbands, fathers and brothers. Gāndhārī confronts Kṛṣṇa and curses the Yādavas. By placing female grief immediately after military victory, the epic makes bereavement and funeral duty—not royal triumph—the first interpretation of Kurukṣetra’s outcome.

12. Śānti Parva — ruling after catastrophe

Yudhiṣṭhira does not want the kingdom won through mass killing. Bhīṣma, still alive on the arrows, answers through the enormous Rājadharma, Āpaddharma and Mokṣadharma collections. Kingship, emergency ethics, gifts, renunciation, self-knowledge, Sāṃkhya- and Yoga-like teaching and the Nārāyaṇīya turn the postwar crisis into the epic’s largest sustained archive of political and liberation thought.

13. Anuśāsana Parva — norms, gifts and Bhīṣma’s death

Bhīṣma continues with instruction on gifts, vows, social duties, non-injury, food, conduct and religious merit. The material is heterogeneous and historically layered, but its placement matters: the victorious king is rebuilt through listening to prescriptive knowledge from a dying member of the defeated generation. Bhīṣma’s eventual death closes the long pedagogical suspension begun in Book 6.

14. Āśvamedhika Parva — sovereignty reconstructed

Yudhiṣṭhira’s horse sacrifice ritualizes restored imperial power while Arjuna follows the horse through conflicts with regional rulers. The book also contains the Anugītā, where Kṛṣṇa does not simply repeat the Bhagavad Gītā but offers new dialogues and remembered teachings. Ritual restoration therefore accompanies acknowledgement that the unique crisis of the battlefield cannot simply be recreated.

15. Āśramavāsika Parva — the elders withdraw

Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Gāndhārī and Kuntī leave the court for forest retirement. Yudhiṣṭhira’s relation to the defeated king shifts from political danger to care and grief. Their deaths in a forest fire remove the older generation and show that renunciation does not erase the consequences of the dynastic disaster.

16. Mausala Parva — destruction of the Yādavas

Gāndhārī’s curse and internal Yādava violence converge at Prabhāsa. Intoxicated quarrel turns reeds into lethal weapons, Balarāma and Kṛṣṇa depart, and Dvārakā’s order collapses. The victors’ divine allies therefore do not found an indefinitely stable postwar age; the epic extends destruction into the very lineage that made Pāṇḍava victory possible.

17. Mahāprasthānika Parva — the great departure

The Pāṇḍavas relinquish the kingdom and walk north with Draupadī and a dog. One companion after another falls, and Yudhiṣṭhira interprets each fall through a residual moral attachment. The royal epic contracts into a final pilgrimage in which no kingdom, weapon or kinship network can be carried onward.

18. Svargārohaṇa Parva — heaven and the last test

Yudhiṣṭhira refuses to abandon the dog, discovers it to be Dharma and then encounters a heaven in which Duryodhana appears before his own brothers. A final vision of hell destabilizes simple reward-and-punishment expectations before the protagonists assume their divine conditions. The epic ends by making even the moral interpretation of its war subject to one last reversal.

Harivaṃśa — the khila and Kṛṣṇa’s genealogy

The Harivaṃśa supplements the dynastic epic with creation and lunar genealogy, Kṛṣṇa’s birth and early deeds, and further divine narratives. Traditional editions are much larger than the critical text; the critical edition constitutes roughly six thousand verses in three parvans. Its attachment to the Mahābhārata helps bridge epic Kṛṣṇa theology and later Purāṇic Krishna biography without making the supplement compositionally identical with the eighteen books.

Interpretation and reception

Dharma under conditions of conflict

The epic rarely allows a maxim to settle a crisis by itself. Yudhiṣṭhira’s commitment to truth coexists with destructive gambling; Bhīṣma’s loyalty sustains a court he knows is failing; Droṇa and Karṇa bind personal debts to an unjust political cause; Kṛṣṇa repeatedly treats rigid adherence to warrior convention as inadequate to an already corrupted war. Dharma is therefore tested through competing obligations, compromised institutions and consequences that outlive intention.

War is not the conclusion

Only four of the eighteen parvas are chiefly devoted to the main battle, while the enormous Śānti and Anuśāsana collections follow it. The architecture makes a philosophical claim: winning the war does not answer how a survivor should rule, mourn, atone, give, renounce or seek liberation. The epic spends more textual energy interpreting catastrophe than celebrating conquest.

Bhagavad Gītā and other embedded texts

The Bhagavad Gītā became the most internationally famous unit, but its setting inside Bhīṣma Parva matters: its account of action and detachment answers Arjuna at the threshold of a specific kin-war. The same principle applies to Nala, Sāvitrī, the Sanatsujātīya, Mokṣadharma and the Nārāyaṇīya. Their independent reception should not erase the narrative problem each addresses inside the epic.

Reception

The Mahābhārata has generated Sanskrit commentaries, vernacular retellings, performance, visual art, political interpretation and modern literature across South and Southeast Asia. Later traditions repeatedly recenter Draupadī, Karṇa, Ekalavya, Gāndhārī and other figures whose ethical force exceeds the amount of narrative space they receive in the dynastic plot.

Critical edition and transmission

The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute’s critical-edition project began under V. S. Sukthankar and was completed in 1966 after decades of manuscript collation. The editors consulted more than a thousand manuscripts in many scripts and grouped readings genealogically rather than privileging one printed vulgate.

The constituted text reconstructs a common transmissional ancestor to the extent permitted by the surviving evidence; it is not “Vyāsa’s original manuscript.” Passages omitted from the main text are preserved in the critical apparatus and remain essential for studying regional recensions, later theology and reception.

The Harivaṃśa received its own critical edition because its manuscript history and scale differ from the eighteen-parva epic. Precise scholarship therefore distinguishes the critical Mahābhārata, regional vulgates and the critical or vulgate Harivaṃśa when quoting chapter and verse.
