The Mahabharata is one of the two great Sanskrit Itihasas and one of the largest epic traditions in world literature. Traditionally attributed to Vyasa, its central narrative follows the Kuru dynasty, the conflict between the Pandavas and Kauravas, and the devastating war at Kurukshetra.

The received work is also an enormous archive of mythology, dharma, political reflection, pilgrimage, ritual and philosophy. Its most famous embedded text is the Bhagavad Gita, but the epic contains many independent narratives and long didactic books whose histories extend beyond the central war story.

Date

The Mahabharata is the product of a long period of oral, literary and scholastic growth. Modern scholarship generally treats it as a layered composition whose major formation belongs to the late first millennium BCE and the early centuries CE, rather than the work of one historical author writing at one time.

The text itself remembers growth from a smaller war narrative into a much larger encyclopedic work. Precise chronological models differ, and individual books contain material of different ages, so dates for particular passages should not automatically be extended to the entire epic.

Structure

The received Mahabharata is organized into 18 parvas, followed in many traditions by the Harivamsha as a supplement. Traditional descriptions characterize the epic as a work of roughly 100,000 shlokas, though counts differ by recension and by what is included.

The 18-book architecture moves from the origins of the Kuru conflict through exile and war to the long aftermath: instruction, grief, royal succession, renunciation and the final journey. Large didactic books, especially the Shanti and Anushasana Parvas, make the epic far more than a war story.

Contents

The central narrative concerns the rivalry between the Pandavas and Kauravas, competing branches of the Kuru dynasty. A rigged dice game sends the Pandavas into exile; failed negotiations eventually lead to the catastrophic war at Kurukshetra.

The epic contains hundreds of embedded narratives and discourses. The Bhagavad Gita, spoken between Krishna and Arjuna before battle, is its most famous philosophical text, but the Mahabharata also preserves extensive materials on kingship, law, pilgrimage, mythology, ethics, liberation and social obligation.

Theology

The Mahabharata repeatedly describes dharma as difficult, contextual and vulnerable to conflict between competing duties. Its heroes rarely face simple choices, and the consequences of the war prevent the text from functioning as a straightforward celebration of victory.

Krishna occupies a central theological role, particularly in the Bhagavad Gita and other Vaishnava passages, while the epic as a whole contains diverse traditions involving Shiva, the Goddess, ascetics, sacrifice, yoga, Samkhya and moksha. Its theological plurality reflects the composite history of the text.

Influence

The Mahabharata became a foundational source for Hindu narrative, political thought, ethics, theology, pilgrimage traditions and performing arts. Episodes such as the Bhagavad Gita, Nala and Damayanti, Savitri, and the story of Shakuntala developed influential lives beyond the epic itself.

It has been translated, retold and performed in nearly every major literary culture of South Asia and in several Southeast Asian traditions. Later reception often reorganizes or theologizes the epic differently, so the history of interpretation is itself part of the work's significance.

Rites

The epic preserves extensive discussions of sacrifice, gifts, vows, pilgrimage, funeral rites, kingship, household duty, asceticism and social order. The Shanti and Anushasana Parvas in particular became important repositories of normative discourse.

These materials are essential evidence for the history of Indian religious and legal thought, but they represent layered prescriptions and debates. They should not be treated as a transparent snapshot of one uniform society.

Critical edition

The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute in Pune produced the major critical edition of the Mahabharata between 1933 and 1966. Beginning under V. S. Sukthankar, the project collated a large manuscript base and attempted to reconstruct the common archetype underlying major regional recensions.

The critical edition is substantially shorter than many vulgate editions because passages judged secondary to the reconstructed archetype are placed in the apparatus or appendices. For that reason, chapter and verse references must identify the edition or recension being cited.
