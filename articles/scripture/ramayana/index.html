Rāmāyaṇa
रामायणम्
TraditionItihāsa / Sanskrit epic
Traditional poetVālmīki
LanguageSanskrit
FormationLayered, chiefly later first millennium BCE into the early centuries CE
Received structure7 kāṇḍas, about 500 sargas
Traditional extentAbout 24,000 verses
Critical editionOriental Institute, Baroda, 1960–1975

The Rāmāyaṇa follows Rāma from a succession crisis at Ayodhyā through fourteen years of exile, Sītā’s abduction, alliance with Sugrīva and Hanumān, the war in Laṅkā and the consequences of restored kingship. The received poem is at once heroic narrative, reflection on royal and household dharma, sacred geography and—especially in its developed frame—a theology of Rāma as Viṣṇu embodied.

Its seven-book form is ancient and authoritative, but it is not compositionally uniform. The strongest continuous exile narrative runs through Ayodhyā, Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Sundara and Yuddha, while Bāla and Uttara contain conspicuous framing, genealogical, theological and retrospective materials whose development is central to modern study of the epic.

Date and formation

The Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa is the result of prolonged oral, literary and scholastic formation rather than one datable act of authorship. Modern estimates for major stages range across the later first millennium BCE and the opening centuries CE. The inherited Rāma story may be older than the Sanskrit verses that survive, while recensional development continued after the poem had acquired its recognizable seven-kāṇḍa form.

John Brockington’s stratigraphic analysis is especially useful because it works from the poem itself: vocabulary, metre, style, narrative contradiction and changing religious ideas. In broad terms, Books 2–6 preserve the strongest older narrative continuum, whereas substantial portions of Bāla and Uttara belong to later framing and expansion. This is a relative model, not a rule that every verse in the middle five books is early or every verse in the outer two is late.

Rāma’s status illustrates the development. Much of the central action depends on a prince constrained by promises, kinship and political obligation. The received Bāla and other theological passages explicitly place his birth inside Viṣṇu’s plan to destroy Rāvaṇa. The human-hero and avatāra perspectives coexist in the authoritative poem but need not have reached their final form at the same stage.

Structure and recensions

The received epic contains seven kāṇḍas and roughly five hundred sargas, with a traditional total of about 24,000 verses. Those figures describe the cultural scale of the work rather than identical counts in every manuscript. Chapter division, wording and the presence of passages vary significantly.

The critical-edition project grouped the manuscript evidence into major regional traditions, including northwestern Śāradā witnesses, northeastern Newari and Maithili material, and southern Grantha, Telugu and Malayalam traditions. Devanāgarī manuscripts occur in more than one textual grouping and do not by themselves define a single “northern recension.”

Bāla supplies the poem’s origin frame, Rāma’s birth and education and the Mithilā marriage; Ayodhyā–Yuddha carries the succession–exile–abduction–alliance–war plot; Uttara gives Rāvaṇa’s prehistory and the post-coronation tragedy of Sītā, Lava and Kuśa. Literary structure and compositional history therefore overlap without being identical.

1. Bāla Kāṇḍa — poem, birth and initiation

Vālmīki receives the outline of Rāma’s life from Nārada, the krauñca lament becomes the first śloka and Lava and Kuśa learn the poem. Daśaratha’s heir-producing rite is then linked to the gods’ plan for Viṣṇu to be born as Rāma. Viśvāmitra removes Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa from court, transmits weapons and sacred histories, and leads them from Tāṭakā and Siddhāśrama to Ahalyā, Mithilā, the breaking of Śiva’s bow, the four dynastic marriages and the encounter with Paraśurāma.

2. Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa — succession and exile

Daśaratha prepares Rāma’s consecration, but Kaikeyī claims two old boons: Bharata’s kingship and Rāma’s fourteen-year exile. Rāma accepts his father’s pledged word; Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa insist on sharing the exile. Daśaratha dies, Bharata condemns the arrangement made in his name and travels to Citrakūṭa, but Rāma refuses to return before the vow ends. The sandals left with Bharata turn absent sovereignty into the political problem that structures the years of exile.

3. Araṇya Kāṇḍa — forest and abduction

The exiles pass through ascetic settlements before settling at Pañcavaṭī. Śūrpaṇakhā’s humiliation leads to the destruction of Khara’s forces; Mārīca’s deer deception separates the household, and Rāvaṇa abducts Sītā after Jatāyu’s failed defense. Rāma’s search through Kabandha and Śabarī moves the epic from the human forest of ascetics and rākṣasas toward the vānaras who will make the rescue possible.

4. Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa — alliance and contested kingship

Hanumān brings Rāma to the exiled Sugrīva, who offers intelligence and manpower in return for recovery of his kingdom. Rāma kills Vālin from concealment, an act the poem itself subjects to argument about kingship, punishment and jurisdiction. Sugrīva’s restoration is followed by the delayed search for Sītā, and Sampāti’s information finally identifies Laṅkā as the destination.

5. Sundara Kāṇḍa — Hanumān in Laṅkā

Hanumān crosses the ocean, enters Laṅkā, searches its palaces and finds Sītā in the aśoka grove resisting Rāvaṇa. Rāma’s ring and Sītā’s token establish recognition across separation. Hanumān then shifts from secret envoy to destructive warrior, is captured, burns Laṅkā with his ignited tail and returns with the evidence that transforms grief into a military objective.

6. Yuddha Kāṇḍa — war, return and public kingship

Vibhīṣaṇa abandons Rāvaṇa, the bridge is built and the allied army enters Laṅkā. Kumbhakarṇa and Indrajit fall before Rāma kills Rāvaṇa and installs Vibhīṣaṇa. Sītā’s public ordeal after rescue exposes a central tension of the epic: private knowledge and marital loyalty do not automatically settle the demands Rāma associates with royal reputation. The return and coronation resolve the war while preparing the problem developed in Uttara.

7. Uttara Kāṇḍa — prehistory and the tragedy of rule

Uttara first looks backward to Rāvaṇa’s ancestry, boons and conquests, then forward from Rāma’s coronation. Public suspicion leads to the pregnant Sītā’s abandonment near Vālmīki’s hermitage, where Lava and Kuśa learn and perform the Rāmāyaṇa before Rāma. Sītā’s return to the earth and Rāma’s eventual departure make the final book a meditation on the destructive cost of kingship, reputation and irreversible vows.

Interpretation and reception

Dharma as collision rather than rule-book

The epic repeatedly places legitimate obligations against one another: Daśaratha’s promise against succession, Rāma’s filial obedience against Kausalyā’s grief, Sītā’s marital claims against Rāma’s attempt to protect her, Sugrīva’s obligation to Rāma against restored royal pleasure, and private trust in Sītā against the politics of public reputation. Dharma is therefore dramatized through cases in which no choice preserves every good.

Rāma, Sītā and models of agency

Rāma’s self-control is one ethical center, but the poem distributes moral argument among many speakers. Sītā repeatedly contests decisions made for her; Bharata refuses an unjust benefit; Lakṣmaṇa questions obedience; Hanumān combines intelligence with service; Vibhīṣaṇa abandons kinship when loyalty to Rāvaṇa becomes indefensible. The epic’s moral world is polyphonic even when the received theology exalts Rāma.

Later Rāmāyaṇas

The Sanskrit epic generated Tamil, Awadhi, Bengali, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, Jain, Buddhist and Southeast Asian traditions. Kampaṉ, Tulsīdās, Jain tellings, Rāmlīlā and regional performance do not merely reproduce Vālmīki: they reorganize theology, character and moral emphasis. “The Rāmāyaṇa” therefore names both one Sanskrit textual tradition and a much larger history of retelling.

Critical edition and transmission

The Oriental Institute at Baroda prepared the seven-volume critical edition between 1960 and 1975 after comparing manuscripts from the principal regional traditions. The editors constituted an earlier recoverable common text and placed many unsupported or secondary passages in the apparatus rather than presenting a single regional vulgate as the original.

A critical edition does not reproduce Vālmīki’s autograph and does not make regional recensions religiously irrelevant. It answers a narrower historical question about recoverable shared transmission. Passages familiar from one regional or later devotional Rāmāyaṇa therefore need to be identified by textual source when they are compared with the critical Sanskrit epic.
