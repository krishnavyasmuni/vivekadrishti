Ramayana रामायणम्

The Ramayana is one of the two great Sanskrit Itihasas. Traditionally attributed to Valmiki, it narrates the exile of Rama, the abduction of Sita by Ravana, the alliance with Hanuman and the vanaras, the war in Lanka and the return to Ayodhya.

The surviving epic is a layered textual tradition with multiple recensions and a vast history of retelling. Its importance extends from Sanskrit literature into theology, ethics, kingship, devotion, performance and the religious cultures of South and Southeast Asia.

Date

The Ramayana is a layered Sanskrit epic rather than a text that can be assigned securely to one historical year. Its narrative core is generally placed in the later first millennium BCE, while linguistic, narrative and theological additions accumulated over a long period of transmission.

Modern scholarship often distinguishes an older narrative core from later strata, especially portions of the Bala Kanda and Uttara Kanda. Exact dates remain debated, so the safest description is a long process of composition and redaction rather than a single moment of authorship.

Structure

The received Sanskrit Valmiki Ramayana is arranged in seven kandas. Traditional descriptions speak of roughly 24,000 verses, although verse and chapter counts vary among recensions and editions.

  • Bala Kanda — Rama's youth, Vishvamitra, Sita's svayamvara and the marriage at Mithila.
  • Ayodhya Kanda — the succession crisis, Kaikeyi's boons and Rama's exile.
  • Aranya Kanda — forest life and Sita's abduction by Ravana.
  • Kishkindha Kanda — alliance with Sugriva and the vanaras.
  • Sundara Kanda — Hanuman's journey to Lanka and discovery of Sita.
  • Yuddha Kanda — the war in Lanka, Ravana's defeat and Rama's return.
  • Uttara Kanda — later reign narratives, Sita's exile and the closing traditions.

Contents

The epic follows Rama, prince of Ayodhya, whose intended coronation is overturned by a palace succession crisis. He enters the forest with Sita and Lakshmana; Sita is abducted by Ravana, king of Lanka, and Rama forms an alliance with Sugriva, Hanuman and the vanara forces.

The journey culminates in the crossing to Lanka, the war against Ravana and Sita's recovery. The received epic then continues into questions of kingship, public reputation, separation and the fate of Rama's household, giving the work a more complicated ending than a simple victory narrative.

Theology

The Ramayana is a major text for reflection on dharma, kingship, kinship, loyalty, renunciation, grief and moral conflict. Rama is simultaneously presented as an exemplary human ruler and, in important strata of the received text and later reception, as an incarnation of Vishnu.

Sita, Hanuman, Lakshmana and Bharata became major religious exemplars in their own right. The epic's theology cannot be reduced to one doctrine: its narrative repeatedly tests duties that conflict with one another and became a foundation for many later Vaishnava, devotional and ethical interpretations.

Influence

The Ramayana generated one of the largest literary and performance traditions in Asia. Sanskrit retellings were joined by major works in Tamil, Hindi, Bengali, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, Marathi and many other languages, as well as Buddhist, Jain and Southeast Asian adaptations.

Its stories became central to temple art, dance, drama, recitation and public festivals. Later retellings often reshape character, theology and emphasis, so they should be read as creative receptions rather than assumed to reproduce every detail of the Valmiki text.

Rites

The epic supplies models of royal duty, family obligation, hospitality, ascetic life, vows, funeral observance and pilgrimage. These passages are important witnesses to normative ideals, but they are literary prescriptions and narrative representations rather than direct demographic records of a single historical society.

In lived tradition, recitation of the Ramayana and especially the Sundara Kanda acquired devotional and ritual importance. Rama-centered festivals, Hanuman devotion and regional performance traditions developed far beyond the boundaries of the Sanskrit epic itself.

Critical edition

The Oriental Institute, Baroda produced the major critical edition of the Valmiki Ramayana in seven volumes between 1960 and 1975. Its editors compared manuscript evidence from different regions in an effort to reconstruct a text earlier than the divergent received recensions.

A critical edition is not identical with every traditional printed Ramayana. Passages familiar from later or regional recensions may appear in the critical apparatus rather than the constituted text, so quotations should identify the edition being used.
