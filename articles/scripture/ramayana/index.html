Ramayana रामायणम्

Ramayana
रामायणम्
TraditionItihasa / Sanskrit epic
Traditional poetValmiki
LanguageSanskrit
FormationLayered; later first millennium BCE to early centuries CE
Received structure7 kandas
Traditional extentAbout 24,000 verses
Critical editionOriental Institute, Baroda, 1960–1975

The Ramayana follows Rama from the threatened succession at Ayodhya through forest exile, Sita’s abduction, alliance with Sugriva and Hanuman, the war in Lanka and the difficult consequences of kingship. Its received seven-book form contains both a sustained heroic narrative and visible layers of framing, theology and later moral reflection.

The contents below follow the seven kandas separately. Structure describes recensions and literary formation; it no longer repeats the plot that belongs in the kanda sections.

Date and formation

No surviving evidence permits the whole seven-book poem to be dated to one act of composition. Linguistic, metrical and narrative study places its formation broadly across the later first millennium BCE and early centuries CE, while distinguishing the age of inherited stories from the Sanskrit verses in which they survive.

John Brockington’s stratigraphic work identifies a strong older narrative movement in books 2–6 and major development in the framing and theological materials of Bala and Uttara. This does not mean that every verse of the middle books is early or every verse of the outer books is late; the model describes relative layers within a received literary whole.

Rama’s identity makes the history visible. Much of the central drama depends on a prince acting under human obligations, while explicit avatara framing makes his birth part of Vishnu’s plan to destroy Ravana. Both perspectives are authoritative in the received epic even if they did not reach their final wording at the same time.

Structure and recensions

The Valmiki Ramayana is transmitted in seven kandas and roughly five hundred sargas, but wording, chapter division and passage extent differ among northern, northwestern and southern manuscript traditions. The traditional total of 24,000 verses names the cultural scale of the epic rather than an identical count in every recension.

Bala introduces Rama’s genealogy, youth and marriage; Ayodhya through Yuddha carries the continuous exile–abduction–alliance–war narrative; Uttara supplies Ravana’s prehistory and the post-coronation tragedy. Treating those books as divisions of one received work is compatible with studying their different compositional profiles.

1. Bala Kanda — Childhood

Valmiki hears the outline of Rama’s life and composes the poem; Dasharatha’s sacrifice leads to the birth of Rama and his brothers. Vishvamitra takes Rama and Lakshmana from court, gives them weapons, has them protect sacrifice and guides them through inherited sacred stories; Rama frees Ahalya, breaks Shiva’s bow at Mithila and marries Sita before confronting Parashurama on the return journey.

2. Ayodhya Kanda — The Succession Crisis

Dasharatha prepares Rama’s installation, but Kaikeyi claims two old boons: Bharata’s kingship and Rama’s fourteen-year exile. Rama accepts his father’s word, Sita and Lakshmana accompany him, Dasharatha dies, and Bharata refuses to profit from his mother’s demand; he rules from Nandigrama with Rama’s sandals as the sign of absent sovereignty.

3. Aranya Kanda — The Forest

The exiles move through ascetic settlements and settle at Panchavati. Shurpanakha’s mutilation leads to the destruction of Khara’s forces; Maricha’s golden-deer deception separates the household, Ravana abducts Sita and kills the defending Jatayu, and Rama’s search brings him through Kabandha to Shabari and toward the vanara world.

4. Kishkindha Kanda — Alliance

Hanuman brings Rama to the exiled Sugriva, who promises help in exchange for recovering his kingdom. Rama kills Vali from concealment, Sugriva is installed, and after the rains the search parties depart; Sampati finally identifies Lanka as Sita’s location, making Hanuman’s crossing possible.

5. Sundara Kanda — Hanuman in Lanka

Hanuman crosses the ocean, searches Lanka and finds Sita in the ashoka grove resisting Ravana. He gives Rama’s ring and receives Sita’s token, then allows himself to be captured after destroying the grove; his burning tail becomes the means of firing Lanka before he returns with proof that Sita lives.

6. Yuddha Kanda — War

Vibhishana leaves Ravana and joins Rama, the bridge is built and the allied army enters Lanka. Kumbhakarna and Indrajit fall, Rama kills Ravana and installs Vibhishana; Sita undergoes a public ordeal before the return to Ayodhya and Rama’s coronation. The book resolves the military plot while leaving the relation between private trust and public kingship unsettled.

7. Uttara Kanda — After the Coronation

The book narrates Ravana’s ancestry and earlier victories, then returns to Rama’s reign. Public suspicion leads to the pregnant Sita’s exile at Valmiki’s hermitage, where Lava and Kusha learn and recite the Ramayana; after Sita calls on the earth to receive her, Rama completes his reign and eventually departs from the world. The ending turns royal reputation into a tragedy that later traditions repeatedly defend, revise or reject.

Theology, influence and practice

Theology and dharma

Rama is both a prince constrained by promise and, in the mature received framework, Vishnu embodied. Dharma emerges through collisions among obedience, marriage, kingship, kinship and justice; Sita, Bharata, Lakshmana, Hanuman and Vibhishana are not decorative supporters but alternative models of fidelity and moral choice.

Influence and reception

The Sanskrit epic generated Tamil, Awadhi, Bengali, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, Jain, Buddhist and Southeast Asian Ramayana traditions. These works alter emphasis, theology and plot as acts of interpretation; performance in Ramlila, dance-drama, shadow theatre and temple recitation gives the story further commentarial lives.

Rites and historical practice

Sacrifice, funeral observance, coronation, forest asceticism, warfare, hospitality and household duty make the epic important for social and religious history. Its prescriptions and narrative ideals belong to different layers, so they cannot be converted into a single factual portrait of all ancient Indian practice; later recitation of Sundara Kanda and Rama devotion belong to reception history and should be identified as such.

Critical edition

The Oriental Institute at Baroda published the seven-volume critical edition from 1960 to 1975. Its editors compared the principal regional manuscript traditions, constituted an earlier recoverable text and preserved rejected or variant passages in the apparatus rather than claiming to reproduce Valmiki’s lost autograph.

The edition provides a common philological baseline, not a verdict against regional Ramayanas. A responsible citation identifies whether it uses the critical text, a regional recension or a later retelling, especially where well-known passages differ.

Further reading

Use the Baroda critical edition and the Princeton Ramayana of Valmiki translation for text and apparatus. John Brockington’s Righteous Rama and The Sanskrit Epics are central for formation history; reception should then be studied within each language, community and performance tradition rather than through a flat list of “versions.”
